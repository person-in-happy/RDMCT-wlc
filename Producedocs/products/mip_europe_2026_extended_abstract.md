## Abstract

Scheduling a rotary semiconductor cluster tool requires discrete batch assignment and continuous-time coordination of robots, load locks, process chambers, and reusable auxiliary wafers. We study a dual-source setting in which product wafers enter from load ports while process-enabling carrier (PEC) wafers enter from a separate finite store. The two streams interact inside two shared rotary chambers under two recipe modes: a four-wafer `4x1` batch and an overlapping `2x2` chain whose adjacent cycles exchange wafer pairs. We formulate an end-to-end mixed-integer linear program that explicitly represents product and PEC routes, serial single-slot alignment, paired robot transfers, stateful load-lock reuse, chamber rotations, empty robot repositioning, PEC-token reuse, and periodic pure-PEC cleaning. The primary objective minimizes the time at which the last product wafer returns to its load port.

The resulting formulation is structurally rich and becomes difficult even at moderate mixed-flow sizes. We therefore introduce a structure-aware hierarchical cut-selection policy within SCIP. Each solver-generated candidate cut is represented by 13 generic quality features and 10 application-structure features that measure coefficient mass over route, `4x1` assignment, `2x2` assignment, timing, completion, and residual variable families. A high-level policy determines the cut budget, a pointer policy proposes an ordered subset, and a deterministic structure-aware layer enforces family coverage while penalizing redundant cuts. This procedure only selects among valid cuts generated by SCIP and therefore does not alter the feasible set. Preliminary same-version experiments solve four-wafer pure-mode and eight-wafer mixed-mode instances to proven optimality. A 16-wafer mixed instance reaches a feasible makespan of 1150 within 60 seconds but retains a 99.98% relative gap, exposing the computational regime targeted by the learned selector. A controlled multi-seed comparison of cut policies is the next required step before making performance claims.

**Keywords:** mixed-integer programming; semiconductor cluster tools; mixed-flow scheduling; cutting planes; learning to optimize; robot scheduling

## 1. Motivation and Related Work

Cluster-tool scheduling is difficult because chamber processing and robot motion must be planned jointly. Classical studies often exploit cyclic operation, predefined robot sequences, or steady-state assumptions. Recent work has broadened this scope through non-cyclic timed-Petri-net formulations, concurrent processing of multiple wafer types, and reinforcement-learning policies for robot and release decisions [1–4]. These advances establish the value of explicit robot scheduling, but they do not directly cover the equipment considered here: product and auxiliary wafers originate from different stores, follow different return paths, and participate jointly in rotary recipes with finite auxiliary-wafer reuse.

The physical distinction between the two recipe modes is central. In `4x1`, two product-wafer (PW) pairs occupy opposite sides of a rotary chamber; loading, rotation, processing, and unloading follow a fixed physical order. In `2x2`, product pairs form a continuous chain between a pure-PEC head and a pure-PEC tail, and an intermediate bridge action exchanges an outgoing pair for an incoming pair. Replacing either mode by an aggregate processing duration can produce schedules that overlap robot motion, overfill a single-slot aligner, omit empty robot returns, or use a PEC wafer simultaneously in two chambers. Our formulation is therefore end-to-end rather than chamber-only.

The second challenge is computational. Modern MILP solvers generate many valid cutting planes and must select a useful subset. Learned cut ranking, adaptive cut selection, and hierarchical sequence models show that cut preference, cardinality, and order can materially affect branch-and-cut performance [5–9]. However, generic cut features do not reveal which physical decision block a cut acts on. In the proposed scheduling formulation, a cut concentrated on route decisions has a different role from one concentrated on rotary-batch assignment or continuous timing. This observation motivates an application-structured cut representation and diversity-aware selection rule.

Our contribution is threefold:

1. We formulate a dual-source, mixed-flow, continuous-time MILP that integrates product wafers, reusable PEC wafers, `4x1` rotary batches, `2x2` exchange chains, robot motion, stateful load locks, and chamber cleaning.
2. We define a 23-dimensional cut representation whose structural part is computed from coefficient mass over physically meaningful variable families.
3. We combine a hierarchical actor–critic-style cut policy with family-coverage quotas and redundancy-aware reranking, while retaining SCIP's validity guarantees because no new cut is generated by the learned component.

## 2. Dual-Source Rotary-Tool Scheduling Problem

### 2.1 Equipment and wafer flows

Let $W=W^{4x1}\cup W^{2x2}$ denote product wafers and let $Q$ denote reusable PEC tokens. A product wafer follows

\[
\mathrm{LP}\rightarrow\mathrm{ATR}\rightarrow\mathrm{AL}\rightarrow
\mathrm{ATR}\rightarrow\mathrm{LL}^{up}\rightarrow\mathrm{VTR}
\rightarrow\mathrm{CH}\rightarrow\mathrm{VTR}\rightarrow
\mathrm{LL}^{low}\rightarrow\mathrm{ATR}\rightarrow\mathrm{LP},
\]

whereas a PEC wafer follows

\[
\mathrm{PEC\ store}\rightarrow\mathrm{VTR}\rightarrow\mathrm{CH}
\rightarrow\mathrm{VTR}\rightarrow\mathrm{PEC\ store}.
\]

ATR and VTR denote the atmospheric and vacuum transfer robots, AL is a one-slot aligner, and each upper and lower load lock has two independently reusable slots. The two chambers, CH2 and CH3, share VTR. Product wafers of the same mode are grouped into PW pairs. An odd product tail becomes a product–PEC pair, while a missing side of the last active `4x1` batch is filled by a pure PEC–PEC pair.

### 2.2 Rotary recipes

An active `4x1` batch contains two PW pairs and executes

\[
\text{front load}\rightarrow 180^\circ\text{ rotation}\rightarrow
\text{back load}\rightarrow\text{process}\rightarrow
\text{back unload}\rightarrow180^\circ\text{ rotation}\rightarrow
\text{front unload}.
\]

The model starts processing exactly when the second side has been loaded. A pure-PEC filler is permitted only on a tail batch, preventing nonproductive all-PEC batches during ordinary production.

A `2x2` chamber chain consists of a pure-PEC head, a sequence of product PW pairs, and a pure-PEC tail. Each product pair participates in two adjacent process cycles. Between consecutive cycles, VTR performs a bridge that removes the outgoing pair and introduces the incoming pair. This chain representation preserves the required overlap while making every exchange and waiting interval explicit.

Periodic cleaning is represented as a separate pure-PEC rotary batch. It occupies the chamber, VTR, and four PEC tokens and separates process epochs after a configurable number of full-chamber cycles. Pure-PEC chamber occupancy is consequently associated with either a required boundary/filler role or cleaning, rather than an unconstrained production state.

## 3. End-to-End MILP Formulation

### 3.1 Assignment and timing variables

Let $P$ be the set of PW pairs, $C=\{2,3\}$ the chambers, $B_c$ the candidate `4x1` batches, and $K_c$ the candidate `2x2` positions of chamber $c$. Binary variables assign each pair to one compatible batch side or chain position. Additional binary variables activate batches, cleaning epochs, paired robot actions, PEC-token assignments, and disjunctive resource orders. For each physical action $a$, continuous variables $s_a$ and $e_a$ denote its start and end. The makespan variable is $C_{\max}$.

Each product pair is assigned exactly once:

\[
\sum_{c\in C}\left(
\sum_{b\in B_c}\sum_{h\in\{1,2\}}x^{4x1}_{p,b,h,c}
+\sum_{k\in K_c}x^{2x2}_{p,k,c}
\right)=1,
\qquad p\in P.
\]

Compatibility constraints exclude assignment to the wrong recipe mode. Batch-occupancy equations enforce two PW pairs in every active `4x1` batch, with a PEC filler when necessary. Prefix constraints make active `2x2` positions contiguous, thereby excluding holes in a chain.

### 3.2 Physical precedence and duration

For each action $a$, the model enforces

\[
e_a=s_a+d_a,
\]

where $d_a$ is the physical processing, rotation, pressure-transition, calibration, or transfer duration. Product-stage precedences connect LP release to final LP return. The one-slot aligner processes the two wafers of a pair serially: ATR places the first wafer, retains its companion, exchanges the wafers after the first calibration, and retrieves the second wafer after its calibration. Thus, paired transport does not imply simultaneous use of two AL slots.

Every loaded ATR or VTR action contains pickup, motion, and placement. Consecutive actions also require an empty reposition when the previous destination differs from the next source. If $a\prec b$ in a robot sequence, then

\[
s_b\ge e_a+\delta(\operatorname{dest}(a),\operatorname{src}(b)),
\]

where $\delta$ is zero only when the robot is already at the next source. This endpoint-aware transition prevents two consecutive `LLupper -> CH2` loads from omitting the required `CH2 -> LLupper` return.

### 3.3 Shared resources, PEC reuse, and objective

For two actions $a$ and $b$ competing for a unary resource, binary order variable $y_{ab}$ activates the standard disjunction

\[
s_b\ge e_a+\delta_{ab}-M(1-y_{ab}),\qquad
s_a\ge e_b+\delta_{ba}-My_{ab}.
\]

The formulation applies this mechanism to ATR, VTR, AL, chamber windows, and slot-specific load-lock state transitions. Each PEC job is assigned to one chamber-compatible token. Jobs assigned to the same token cannot overlap from VTR pickup at the PEC store until VTR return.

The primary problem is

\[
\min C_{\max},\qquad
C_{\max}\ge C_w^{\mathrm{return}}\qquad(w\in W).
\]

After the primary solve, an optional second phase fixes $C_{\max}$ to the incumbent or proven optimum and minimizes schedule-instability terms such as avoidable chamber gaps and excessive nonprocessing residence. This lexicographic construction prevents compactness penalties from degrading throughput.

## 4. Structure-Aware Hierarchical Cut Selection

### 4.1 Candidate-cut representation

SCIP generates a candidate set $\mathcal C_t$ at separation round $t$. The learned component selects and orders a subset; it does not generate inequalities. For cut $i$, the first 13 features contain objective parallelism, efficacy, support, integral support, normalized violation, four cut-coefficient statistics, and four objective-coefficient statistics.

The structural features partition model variables into

\[
\mathcal G=\{\text{route},\text{full},\text{mix},\text{timing},
\text{completion},\text{other}\}.
\]

For a cut $\sum_j a_{ij}x_j\le b_i$, its coefficient-mass share in family $g$ is

\[
p_{ig}=\frac{\sum_{j\in g}|a_{ij}|}
{\sum_j|a_{ij}|+\varepsilon}.
\]

The 10 structural features are the six values $p_{ig}$, binary and continuous coefficient-mass ratios, the dominant-family ratio, and normalized family entropy

\[
H_i=-\frac{\sum_{g:p_{ig}>0}p_{ig}\log p_{ig}}
{\log |\mathcal G|}.
\]

These quantities expose whether a cut is concentrated on one physical block or couples several blocks.

### 4.2 Hierarchical policy and structural reranking

The high-level stochastic policy predicts a fraction of candidate cuts to retain, which is converted into a bounded integer budget $k_t$. The low-level pointer policy then proposes an ordered list of $k_t$ candidates; beam decoding can replace greedy decoding at test time. Parallel solver trajectories provide policy-gradient samples, and a value or moving-average baseline reduces variance. Because the current implementation aggregates parallel trajectories before updating the policy, we describe it conservatively as an A3C-inspired hierarchical actor–critic policy rather than claiming a strict asynchronous A3C implementation.

The learned order is followed by deterministic structure-aware reranking. A base score combines normalized efficacy, violation, integral support, structured coefficient mass, dominant-family concentration, and an entropy penalty. Family demand in the leading candidate window determines a quota for route, `4x1`, `2x2`, timing, and completion cuts. Remaining positions are filled by maximizing

\[
\Delta(i\mid S)=B_i-\lambda\max_{j\in S}\operatorname{sim}(i,j)
-\mu\,[n_{g_i}(S)+1-Q_{g_i}]_+,
\]

where $S$ is the current selection, $B_i$ is the base score, $Q_{g_i}$ is the family quota, and similarity is the cosine similarity of generic quality and structural-family features. The mechanism retains the policy's cardinality budget while reducing redundant concentration on one model block.

## 5. Preliminary Computational Validation

### 5.1 Protocol

The formulation is implemented in Python 3.13.5 with PySCIPOpt 6.1.0. All preliminary instances use two chambers, eight reusable PEC wafers, a 110-second `4x1` process time, a 50-second `2x2` cycle time, a 2-second half rotation, and a 4-second VTR pair-transfer time. The table reports one deterministic run per case on the current development machine. Model sizes are measured before presolve. These runs validate formulation behavior; they are not evidence that the learned selector outperforms SCIP.

The small mixed case is already substantially harder than either pure case, although it contains only eight product wafers. This behavior supports studying cut management on the mixed formulation, but it does not by itself establish that learning-based selection is beneficial.

### 5.2 Required controlled cut-selection study

The workshop poster should report a common-budget comparison over at least 20 random seeds and multiple train/test instances. The minimum baseline set is: SCIP default selection; no optional cuts; random selection with the same budget; efficacy ranking; fixed-ratio pointer selection; hierarchical learned selection without structural reranking; structure-only reranking; and the full method. Each method must use identical SCIP seeds, time limits, presolve, separation frequency, warm start, and candidate caps.

The primary solver metrics are shifted-geometric-mean solving time, primal–dual integral, node count, final gap, time to first feasible schedule, and optimality rate. Scheduling metrics are makespan, wafer throughput, chamber utilization, ATR/VTR utilization, and total nonprocessing chamber residence. Inference and feature-extraction time must be reported separately, because an improved node count is not useful if policy overhead dominates runtime. Generalization should be evaluated across unseen wafer counts, `4x1`:`2x2` ratios, PEC-pool sizes, cleaning intervals, and process-time distributions.

## 6. Limitations and Planned Extensions

The current evidence is formulation-level rather than algorithm-level. First, the repository does not yet contain a controlled post-VTR-revision, multi-seed comparison that supports a speedup claim for the learned selector. Second, the default runner disables learned cut selection on pure `2x2` and dense mixed instances as a runtime safeguard; this safeguard must be overridden in controlled experiments and replaced by a stable policy before the dense regime can support the learning claim. Third, the current parameters are synthetic engineering values rather than an anonymized production trace. Finally, the model is deterministic and does not yet address robot failures, stochastic process times, or online rescheduling.

These limitations define a concrete validation path. The immediate priority is to establish whether structural features and family quotas improve branch-and-cut performance beyond the hierarchical policy alone. A subsequent extension will compare the formulation against a discrete-event schedule checker and evaluate robustness under process-time perturbations.

## 7. Conclusion

We presented an end-to-end MILP for a dual-source rotary semiconductor cluster tool and a structure-aware mechanism for selecting solver-generated cuts. The scheduling model captures interactions that are lost in chamber-level abstractions, including serial alignment, paired robot motion, empty repositioning, stateful load locks, reusable PEC tokens, mixed rotary recipes, and cleaning. The cut policy complements generic quality features with coefficient-mass information tied to physical decision families. Preliminary experiments verify small instances and reveal a computational cliff in mixed production. The remaining empirical task is a fair, multi-seed comparison that isolates the contribution of learned cardinality, ordered subset selection, and structural reranking.
