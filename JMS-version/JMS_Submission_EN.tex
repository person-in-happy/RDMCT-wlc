\author[ustb]{Lizhen Shao\corref{cor1}}
\ead{lshao@ustb.edu.cn}
\author[ustb]{Lincong Wei}
\author[ustb]{Wei Wang}

\begin{abstract}
Advanced manufacturing processes place increasing demands on the throughput, space utilization, and operational stability of thin-film deposition systems, motivating the development of cluster tools with multi-position rotating chambers. Difficult-to-remove residues from carbon-containing film processes expose unoccupied positions to contamination during processing and cleaning, requiring auxiliary wafers to fill those positions. Auxiliary and product wafers are stored in different regions and form mixed flows through shared robots and multi-position rotating chambers. We develop a two-stage mixed-integer linear programming model. Stage one reduces product-batch makespan subject to residency limits and other physical constraints; stage two preserves its upper bound and principal structure while reducing waiting and variation in consecutive production-cycle intervals. The solution approach combines structured initialization, time-limited MILP search, and timing refinement, and evaluates the role of learned cut selection. In the historical fixed-layout benchmark, the optimal full-occupancy process modes for a 16-product lot are pure $4\times1$, mixed, and pure $2\times2$ operation at cleaning thresholds of 3, 4, and 6, respectively. Cold starts for four configurations with three seeds each obtain no feasible solution within 300~s, whereas main solves with precomputed initial solutions all yield valid schedules. Equal-budget refinement over eight configurations preserves product makespan and reduces total waiting by 22.29\%--50.25\%. The study provides a common modeling and evaluation basis for mode comparison and feasible scheduling within limited computation budgets for rotating-chamber cluster tools under full-occupancy processing.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD) is used in semiconductor thin-film fabrication. Cluster tools (CTs) connect process modules, robots, and load locks into a continuous wafer-handling route. Processing and transport times, load-lock switching, cleaning, and residency constraints jointly determine feasible operation and equipment output \citep{pan2018survey}. Three-dimensional device structures and multilayer integration increase demands on deposition uniformity and processing capability \citep{appliedprecisioncvd}. Multi-space process modules and multi-finger robots provide a structural basis for parallel processing within a tool \citep{gu2024applied,gu2024multifinger}.

This study considers a PECVD CT with four-position rotating chambers. The operating decision starts from the full-occupancy requirement of the studied recipes: all four positions must contain wafers during production, with dummy wafers filling positions not occupied by products. We use auxiliary wafer to denote a reusable dummy wafer used for production filling or cleaning occupancy. Dummies do not contribute to product output, but their handling, rotation, transport, and repeated loans consume resources. Products enter from atmospheric load ports through alignment and load locks; in dual-source operation, auxiliaries come from vacuum storage. These source and route differences form part of the equipment conditions for mode comparison.

Full occupancy couples product supply with auxiliary reuse. Auxiliary wafers fill positions when products cannot occupy the entire chamber. Cleaning occupies auxiliary wafers and chambers and changes their subsequent availability. Product arrival therefore does not alone permit processing: auxiliary availability, robot service order, and cleaning boundaries must also be satisfied. The two wafer routes in Fig.~\ref{fig:system-en-new} require coordinated scheduling.

Two prescribed modes are considered: $4\times1$ parallel processing gives four wafers one synchronous exposure, whereas $2\times2$ chained processing uses paired transfers and rotation to give each product two consecutive exposures. Recipes and chamber operations define these modes. Dummy wafers required for full occupancy give them different resource costs for partial batches, chain initialization and termination, and cleaning transitions. Equal cumulative nominal processing time per product therefore need not yield equal batch makespan. Mode preference must be assessed against lot size, cleaning threshold, dummy supply, and handling conditions. Product modes are fixed inputs within each solve. Mode selection is supported by separately constructing pure and mixed configurations and comparing validated schedules and bounds, rather than freely changing product recipes within one solve.

CT studies established analytical relationships between processing, handling, and throughput \citep{perkinson1994throughput} and used linear programming, heuristics, and timed Petri nets to obtain schedules under residency constraints \citep{rostami2002optimal,wu2010closedform,qiao2012sojourn}. Later models address revisiting processes, multiple wafer types, and selectable modules \citep{qiao2014revisiting,wang2020multiple,wang2025virtualcell}. Clean/dirty-hand separation and steady-state analysis of four-finger robots and four-space modules, together with joint optimization of two-type wafer releases and handling, supports more complex configurations \citep{gu2024multifinger,yang2025miptwowafers}. Finite batches additionally involve chain initialization, termination, and partial-batch filling, making auxiliary return times direct constraints on subsequent processing.

Periodic-cleaning scheduling uses task-sequence enumeration, partial loading and timing control, virtual-wafer occupancy, and chamber-state-dependent triggers to coordinate maintenance and production \citep{kim2013cleaning,yu2020delay,qiao2022binary,li2022conditioncleaning}. Shared-buffer synchronization and dynamic action screening address deadlock during failure shutdown and noncyclic operation \citep{zhu2025failuredeadlock,kim2025noncyclicdeadlock}; waiting constraints also affect equipment output and upstream control \citep{lee2018waiting}. Here, cleaning simultaneously changes chain segmentation, auxiliary occupancy, and vacuum-robot order. Scheduling product processing or cleaning independently would omit these dependencies.

Timing quality requires distinguishing batch efficiency from coordination between operations. JMS research links waiting limits to effective output \citep{lee2018waiting}, while JIT scheduling seeks completion times aligned with downstream acceptance \citep{weng2022jit}. For the present tool, reducing avoidable waiting and variation in repeated production-cycle intervals can support predictable supply and pickup plans. However, timely demand-driven coordination, uniform wafer residency, and regular cycle-start intervals are distinct criteria. This study directly optimizes waiting and cycle cadence and imposes hard residency limits; these measures do not establish equal residency or strict JIT control.

Two hardware-deviation mechanisms motivate schedule updates: actual action durations can deviate from nominal values, and chambers or robots can differ in performance. Either can change resource availability and require mode evaluation and scheduling to be recomputed for updated parameters. JMS research on uncertain durations explicitly distinguishes proactive scheduling from reactive adjustment \citep{rahmani2014robust}. Here, the computational task is time-limited scheduling for a given parameter snapshot: obtain a complete constraint-checked schedule within the budget, then improve makespan and timing quality. Current experiments do not inject online stochastic deviations or implement measurement feedback and closed-loop rescheduling; they validate scheduling only for the tested deterministic configurations.

These wafer and resource constraints define a mixed-integer scheduling problem. Learning can select CT actions directly or assist mathematical optimization. Reinforcement learning with look-ahead search supports noncyclic CT control \citep{kim2025adaptivecluster}; reinforcement-learning selection, cut ranking, and look-ahead imitation select candidate inequalities in MILP \citep{tang2020cut,huang2022cutranking,paulus2022looking}. HEM jointly determines cut counts and ordered sequences \citep{wang2023hem}. We retain this hierarchical decomposition and use equipment-variable structure for cut selection while representing process feasibility in the MILP.

The model and solution approach address which process mode to use under specified operating conditions and how to obtain a valid schedule in time. The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item A joint model of product and dummy resource occupancy connects process modes to batch performance through wafer pairing, auxiliary loans, chamber assignment, complete production chains, and cleaning boundaries, supporting comparisons of pure and mixed configurations under matched equipment conditions.
\item A makespan-first two-stage MILP reduces product-batch completion time within residency limits, then reduces waiting and cycle-interval variation while preserving the makespan bound and principal structure. Operating efficiency, timing quality, and residency uniformity are distinguished explicitly.
\item A solution workflow combines SPBS initialization, time-limited MILP search, and timing refinement. Feasible-schedule availability, product makespan, waiting, and bound quality separately measure its performance, with matched-budget comparisons testing the incremental contribution of learned cut selection.
\end{enumerate}

Section~2 defines wafer routes, process constraints, and the two-stage model. Section~3 presents initialization and cut selection. Section~4 analyzes mode choice, schedule quality, and computational performance. Section~5 concludes.

\section{Problem description and two-stage scheduling model}
\setcounter{equation}{0}

All material considered here consists of wafers: product wafers and auxiliary wafers. Products receive their prescribed recipe; auxiliary wafers fill production positions or occupy the chamber during cleaning and are reused after return. A chamber operation is either production or cleaning. Production means recipe processing in the $4\times1$ or $2\times2$ mode; cleaning uses only auxiliary wafers. One production or cleaning operation constitutes one chamber cycle. A production cycle specifically denotes a recipe-processing cycle and excludes cleaning. Loading, unloading, and rotation are separate actions; waiting is the interval between consecutive actions.

Two decision levels are distinguished. Mode comparison constructs pure $4\times1$, pure $2\times2$, and prescribed mixtures once product-recipe requirements are qualified, holding common conditions such as lot size, source routes, dummy inventory, cleaning rule, and equipment parameters fixed. Scheduling within each configuration jointly determines wafer pairing, chamber assignment, chamber cycles, shared-resource order, and action times for its prescribed recipes. Whether the alternative recipes meet the same product-quality requirements is an input qualification condition; scheduling results do not establish recipe equivalence.

\paragraph{Mode-selection criterion.} Each candidate configuration must satisfy complete routes, full occupancy, resource capacities, and residency limits before its schedule is compared under matched computational settings. A smaller feasible makespan identifies a better schedule obtained within the current budget. Establishing mode dominance additionally requires its feasible upper bound to lie below all competing valid lower bounds, or comparison of proven optima. Overlapping bounds leave the ranking unresolved; timeout without an incumbent does not establish physical infeasibility. For similar product makespans, waiting, cadence, and auxiliary-occupancy characteristics further describe operating costs without changing the optimization priorities used here.

Inputs comprise action durations, resource capacities, residency limits, cleaning threshold, and auxiliary inventory. Reducing batch completion time means reducing the last product-return time $C_{\max}$, rather than changing recipe-prescribed processing durations or minimizing the sum of action durations. Stage one prioritizes $C_{\max}$; stage two improves waiting and production cadence while preserving its bound. Stable coordination is therefore an engineering aim of timing refinement, while equal residency or fixed cadence is not a prerequisite enforced before stage one in the current model. A feasible schedule specifies wafers, resources, and event times for checking precedence, conflicts, and operating metrics.

The cleaning threshold satisfies $H\ge2$, and the shared inventory contains at least four auxiliary wafers. Action durations are deterministic within each solve. No buffers are assumed beyond LPs, aligners, load locks, rotating chambers, and vacuum storage.

All events use start and end times $s_o,e_o$. For a fixed-duration physical action, $e_o=s_o+d_ob_o$ can be substituted directly. For a resource-holding interval spanning several actions, the endpoints are linked to the corresponding pickup and placement events, and its duration is not prescribed. Loading, unloading, rotation, cycle, and cleaning-stage times are indexed aliases of these physical events, not independent clocks.

\subsection{Single- and dual-source physical routes}
\label{sec:model-path-en}

Let $\mathcal W$ be the product-wafer set and $\mathcal I$ the set of wafer-route tasks. Each product has one complete route; each loan of an auxiliary wafer has one supply-and-return route. One physical auxiliary wafer can serve several successive loan tasks.

Let $\mathcal S=\{A,V\}$ denote atmospheric and vacuum source regions. The source configuration is $\sigma\in\{0,1\}$ for single- and dual-source supply, respectively. LP1--LP4 specify product locations within the atmospheric region; each product returns to its original LP. Products satisfy $\chi_{wA}=1,\chi_{wV}=0$. Auxiliary task $i$ satisfies $\chi_{iA}=1-\sigma,\chi_{iV}=\sigma$. Source-membership parameters obey
\begin{equation}
\sum_{s\in\mathcal S}\chi_{is}=1,
\qquad i\in\mathcal I.
\label{eq:source-membership-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:source-membership-en} assigns a source region to every route task. Under single-source supply, products occupy LP1--LP4 and auxiliary wafers are stored beside LP4 in the atmospheric region. Both share ATR and load-lock interfaces, but only products require AL alignment. Under dual-source supply, products still originate at LP1--LP4, whereas auxiliary wafers originate in vacuum storage. Source configuration concerns the regions supplying the two wafer types, not the number of LPs.

In both configurations, products follow LP--ATR--AL--ATR--upper load lock--VTR--process chamber--VTR--lower load lock--ATR--original LP. Alignment precedes the load-lock pressure transition and execution of the prescribed recipe in the chamber.

Single-source auxiliary wafers travel from storage beside LP4 through ATR, the upper load lock, and VTR to the chamber, and return through VTR, the lower load lock, and ATR. A separately supplied auxiliary wafer bypasses AL. During an accompanying entry with a product, it remains on ATR while the product is aligned, and both enter the lock after product retrieval. Neither route aligns the auxiliary wafer.

Dual-source auxiliary wafers travel from vacuum storage through VTR to the chamber and return through VTR after use. Figures~\ref{fig:single-source-en} and \ref{fig:system-en-new} show both configurations. These routes specify the required physical stages; paired-transfer choices and their timing conditions are given in Section~\ref{sec:model-time-en}.

\subsection{Assignments of wafers, PW pairs, chambers, and cycles}
\label{sec:model-assignment-en}

Individual wafers are first organized into PW pairs, which are assigned to compatible chambers and chamber cycles. Production and cleaning use the same candidate-cycle set. Exposure counts and cleaning triggers are specified in Section~\ref{sec:model-process-en}.

\noindent\textbf{Wafer pairing and chamber selection.}
A wafer pair (PW pair) contains two products, one product and one auxiliary wafer, or two auxiliary wafers. Product LP $\ell_w$ and recipe $m_w$ are given; same-recipe products may be paired across LPs. The model chooses the members of each candidate pair $p\in\mathcal P$. Binary $a_{wpr}$ assigns product $w$ to member position $r\in\{0,1\}$, $d_{pr}$ indicates auxiliary occupancy, and $v_p$ activates the pair:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{p,r}a_{wpr}&=1 &&(w\in\mathcal W),\\
\sum_w a_{wpr}+d_{pr}&=v_p &&(p\in\mathcal P,\ r=0,1).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:joint-membership-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:joint-membership-en} assigns every product to exactly one pair and fills both positions of every active pair.

Let $\mathcal M=\{\zeta,\psi,\mathrm{cl}\}$ denote $4\times1$ production, $2\times2$ production, and cleaning. The set $\mathcal C(m)$ contains chambers compatible with operation type $m$. Binary variables $\mu_{pm}$ and $z_{pc}$ choose the pair's operation type and chamber and prohibit incompatible combinations:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\sum_m\mu_{pm}&=v_p, &\sum_c z_{pc}&=v_p,\\
a_{wpr}&\le\mu_{p,m_w},\\
z_{pc}+\mu_{pm}&\le1 &&(c\notin\mathcal C(m)).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:joint-chamber-en}
\end{equation}
Product recipe $m_w$ and source configuration $\sigma$ are inputs; pairing, chamber assignment, and operation order are decisions. Binary $B_p$ identifies a product-containing pair and $B_p^{\psi}$ a pair containing $2\times2$ products:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
B_p&\le\sum_{w,r}a_{wpr}\le2B_p,\qquad B_p+\mu_{p,\mathrm{cl}}\le1,\\
B_p^{\psi}&\le B_p,\quad B_p^{\psi}\le\mu_{p\psi},\quad
B_p^{\psi}\ge B_p+\mu_{p\psi}-1.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:pair-type-en}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Candidate-capacity bounds.}
Auxiliary-only pairs provide filling or cleaning; product-containing pairs cannot perform cleaning. Let $N=|\mathcal W|=N_{\zeta}+N_{\psi}$ be the number of products, excluding reusable auxiliary wafers. Here $N_{\zeta},N_{\psi}$ count products of the two recipes. The following conservative capacities reserve enough indices for joint pairing and cycle decisions:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
T=N_{\zeta}+2N_{\psi},\qquad J_{\max}=T,\\
P_{\max}=N+T+2J_{\max},\qquad Q_{\max}=T+J_{\max}.
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:candidate-bounds-en}
\end{equation}
The bound $T$ limits the total number of production cycles across the tool. Each $4\times1$ product receives one exposure and each $2\times2$ product receives two; every production cycle contains at least one product. Thus the total number of production cycles is at most $N_{\zeta}+2N_{\psi}$. The threshold $H$ limits the production count in each chamber since its initially clean state or latest cleaning. Cleaning is optional at a valid boundary after 1 to $H-1$ production cycles and mandatory when the count reaches $H$. Initial empty cleaning and consecutive cleanings are prohibited, so each cleaning is preceded by at least one production cycle. Hence $J_{\max}=T$ safely bounds total cleanings. This bound retains all valid early-cleaning choices; it does not require $T$ cleanings.

The capacity $P_{\max}$ bounds the candidate pair set $\mathcal P=\{1,\ldots,P_{\max}\}$ for the entire lot. There are at most $N$ product-containing pairs because each contains a product and every product belongs to exactly one pair. Each production cycle contains two PW pairs and at least one product-containing pair, giving at most $T$ auxiliary-only support pairs. Each cleaning uses two auxiliary-only pairs, giving at most $2J_{\max}$ cleaning pairs. Their sum yields $P_{\max}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:candidate-bounds-en}; actual pair activation satisfies $\sum_{p\in\mathcal P}v_p\le P_{\max}$.

Each PW pair denotes one uninterrupted occupancy from loading to unloading. A product-containing $2\times2$ pair participates in two adjacent production cycles but uses one pair index. A physical auxiliary wafer can be borrowed by another pair after return, so candidate-pair capacity differs from physical auxiliary inventory. The bound $Q_{\max}=T+J_{\max}$ limits total production-plus-cleaning cycles across the tool and also supplies the candidate capacity for each chamber.

\noindent\textbf{Pair-to-cycle assignments.}
Let $\mathcal Q_c=\{1,\ldots,Q_{\max}\}$ be the candidate cycles of chamber $c$, with types $4\times1$, $2\times2$, and cleaning. The variable $K_{cqm}$ selects type, and $U_{cq}=\sum_mK_{cqm}\in\{0,1\}$ abbreviates cycle activation. The variable $X_{pcq}$ selects participating pairs, and $X^P_{pcq}$ identifies occupancy by a product-containing pair. Each active cycle contains two pairs with compatible type and chamber:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\sum_mK_{cqm}&=U_{cq}, &\sum_pX_{pcq}&=2U_{cq},\\
X_{pcq}&\le z_{pc}, &X_{pcq}+\mu_{pm}&\le1+K_{cqm},\\
\sum_pX^P_{pcq}&\ge K_{cq\zeta}+K_{cq\psi}, &U_{c,q+1}&\le U_{cq},\\
\sum_{c,q}U_{cq}&\le Q_{\max}, &\sum_{c,q}K_{cq,\mathrm{cl}}&\le J_{\max}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cycle-capacity-en}
\end{equation}
The product-occupancy indicator is linked linearly by
\begin{equation}
X^P_{pcq}\le X_{pcq},\quad X^P_{pcq}\le B_p,\quad
X^P_{pcq}\ge X_{pcq}+B_p-1.
\label{eq:cycle-products-en}
\end{equation}
The indicators $B_p,B_p^{\psi},X^P_{pcq}$ and the later $A^{\psi}_{pcq},Z^{\psi}_{pcq}$ are logical auxiliaries determined by membership, type, and occupancy. Linear linking constraints are retained to avoid products of binary variables. Production cycles therefore contain a product pair, whereas cleaning cycles contain only auxiliary pairs. Prefix activation removes empty cycle indices without prescribing the order of operation types.

\subsection{Action timing and precedence logic}
\label{sec:model-time-en}

Given physical routes and pairing relationships, activation, durations, precedence, and synchronization describe route feasibility and technical action order. Orders chosen to resolve shared-resource conflicts are addressed in Section~\ref{sec:model-resource-en}.

\noindent\textbf{Action activation and duration.}
For candidate action $o\in\mathcal O$, let $b_o$ indicate activation, $s_o,e_o$ be start and end times, $d_o$ be its prescribed duration, and $M$ be a valid large constant. For a fixed-duration physical action, the end time follows from the start time and activation:
\begin{equation}
0\le s_o\le Mb_o,\qquad
0\le e_o\le Mb_o,\qquad
e_o=s_o+d_ob_o.
\label{eq:operation-time-en}
\end{equation}
Inactive actions have zero times; active actions last $d_o$. This form covers robot transfers, alignment, pumping, venting, rotation, recipe processing, auxiliary-wafer handling, and cleaning.

\noindent\textbf{Route-specific precedence.}
Let $\mathcal A^{\rm int}$ contain common chamber-internal precedence arcs, and let $\mathcal A_{is}(\gamma)$ contain the supply-and-return arcs for route task $i$, source region $s$, and transfer choices $\gamma$. The latter collectively denote paired transfers and single-source accompanying entry. The complete arc set is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal A=\mathcal A^{\rm int}\cup
\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal I}\ \bigcup_{\substack{s\in\mathcal S:\\ \chi_{is}=1}}\mathcal A_{is}(\gamma).
\label{eq:route-union-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:route-union-en} activates the route appropriate to each source and transfer choice and connects product routes, auxiliary supply/return routes, and chamber action chains.

For each required precedence arc $(o,o')\in\mathcal A$,
\begin{equation}
b_{o'}\le b_o,\qquad
s_{o'}\ge e_o+\delta_{oo'}-M(2-b_o-b_{o'}).
\label{eq:precedence-en}
\end{equation}
A successor can execute only if its predecessor is active. When both are active, the successor starts after predecessor completion and the minimum interval $\delta_{oo'}$. These constraints connect wafer routes and loading--operation--unloading chains.

\noindent\textbf{Stage links and paired-action synchronization.}
An individual wafer's stage must coincide with the stage selected for its batch, chain position, or cleaning task. For selection indicator $x$ and reference interval $[s^{\rm ref},e^{\rm ref}]$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
-M(1-x)&\le s_o-s^{\rm ref}\le M(1-x),\\
-M(1-x)&\le e_o-e^{\rm ref}\le M(1-x).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:stage-link-en}
\end{equation}
When $x=1$, the individual action and reference stage coincide; when $x=0$, this candidate link is inactive. The link connects pair positions, product routes, auxiliary requests, and chamber operation windows in one time model.

Let $w_1,w_2$ be the two wafers in pair $p$. The variable $\gamma_{pa}$ selects a paired transfer on an eligible atomic transfer leg $a$ at a common location; $s_{wa},e_{wa}$ denote wafer $w$'s start and end times on that leg. Paired transfer requires synchronization:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
-M(1-\gamma_{pa})&\le s_{w_1a}-s_{w_2a}\le M(1-\gamma_{pa}),\\
-M(1-\gamma_{pa})&\le e_{w_1a}-e_{w_2a}\le M(1-\gamma_{pa}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:double-transfer-en}
\end{equation}
When $\gamma_{pa}=1$, the robot transfers the complete pair in one nonpreemptive action. Otherwise, the two wafer actions still use the same robot and are ordered by Eq.~\eqref{eq:resource-order-en}. Actuator exclusivity counts coordinated actions: the two synchronized wafer events form one action and are not ordered against each other, while their common action remains exclusive with other actions. Each wafer retains its own hand-occupancy interval, so synchronization does not merge hand capacity.

\noindent\textbf{Entry logic for a single-source product--auxiliary pair.}
Let $w_1$ be the product and $w_2$ the auxiliary wafer. The variable $\gamma_{p,\mathrm{in}}$ chooses accompanying entry after both are collected. This route link does not require simultaneous pickup from different storage positions. The event labels $\mathrm{pick,ALput,ALpick,LLput}$ denote source pickup, placement on AL, retrieval from AL, and placement into the upper load lock. The indicators $\gamma^{\rm early}_p$ and $\gamma^{\rm late}_p$ choose direct auxiliary entry before product alignment or auxiliary pickup after product placement on AL. Exactly one of these and accompanying entry is selected:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\gamma^{\rm early}_p+\gamma^{\rm late}_p+\gamma_{p,\mathrm{in}}=1,\\
\gamma^{\rm early}_p=1\Rightarrow e_{w_2,\mathrm{LLput}}\le s_{w_1,\mathrm{ALput}},\\
\gamma^{\rm late}_p=1\Rightarrow s_{w_2,\mathrm{pick}}\ge e_{w_1,\mathrm{ALput}},\\
\gamma_{p,\mathrm{in}}=1\ \Longrightarrow
\begin{cases}
e_{w_2,\mathrm{pick}}\le s_{w_1,\mathrm{ALput}},\\
s_{w_2,\mathrm{LLput}}=s_{w_1,\mathrm{LLput}},\\
e_{w_2,\mathrm{LLput}}=e_{w_1,\mathrm{LLput}},\\
s_{w_1,\mathrm{LLput}}\ge e_{w_1,\mathrm{ALpick}}.
\end{cases}\end{gathered}
\label{eq:single-source-accompany-en}
\end{equation}
For accompanying entry, ATR holds the auxiliary wafer before the product is placed on AL, and both enter the load lock together after alignment. The auxiliary wafer occupies one ATR hand from source pickup through completion of load-lock placement. The product occupies AL rather than an ATR hand during alignment and resumes hand occupancy after retrieval. Separate auxiliary supply directly links its pickup and load-lock placement. Neither option activates an AL-alignment event for the auxiliary wafer.

\subsection{Production modes and cleaning}
\label{sec:model-process-en}

Section~\ref{sec:model-assignment-en} determines which pairs occupy each cycle. This section specifies exposure counts, retention and departure between cycles, rotation and transfer order, and cleaning boundaries. All times use the physical-action representation in Section~\ref{sec:model-time-en}.

\noindent\textbf{Production modes and continuous occupancy.}
A regular all-product $4\times1$ batch contains two product PW pairs. Four products receive one synchronous exposure and leave in last-in--first-out order; auxiliary wafers fill shortages. In $2\times2$, each product-containing pair occupies two adjacent production cycles in the same chamber, giving every product two exposures. For three product pairs, a leading auxiliary pair D is loaded, the chamber rotates, and P1 is loaded for production on D+P1. Rotation and chain exchanges then produce P1+P2, P2+P3, and P3+D. P1, P2, and P3 each receive two exposures; the trailing auxiliary pair preserves four-position occupancy. Figures~\ref{fig:chain-en} and \ref{fig:parallel-en} show the sequences.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.93\linewidth]{fig_chain_en_nature}
\caption{$2\times2$ chained production. Auxiliary pair D preserves full occupancy at the chain head and tail. Rotation and exchanges make each product pair participate in two adjacent production cycles, giving every product two exposures.}
\label{fig:chain-en}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_parallel_en_nature}
\caption{$4\times1$ parallel production. Two PW pairs are loaded successively around a rotation, filling all four positions for one synchronous exposure. Pairs leave in last-in--first-out order; auxiliary wafers fill product shortages.}
\label{fig:parallel-en}
\end{figure}

Binary $A_{pcq},Z_{pcq}$ identify the first and last cycles occupied by pair $p$ in chamber $c$. With $X_{pc0}=X_{pc,Q_{\max}+1}=0$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
A_{pcq}&\ge X_{pcq}-X_{pc,q-1}, &A_{pcq}&\le X_{pcq},\\
A_{pcq}&\le1-X_{pc,q-1},\\
Z_{pcq}&\ge X_{pcq}-X_{pc,q+1}, &Z_{pcq}&\le X_{pcq},\\
Z_{pcq}&\le1-X_{pc,q+1},\\
\sum_{c,q}X_{pcq}&=v_p+B_p^{\psi}, &\sum_{c,q}A_{pcq}&=\sum_{c,q}Z_{pcq}=v_p,\\
\sum_pA^{\psi}_{pcq}&\le1, &\sum_pZ^{\psi}_{pcq}&\le1.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cycle-exposures-en}
\end{equation}
Here $A^{\psi}_{pcq}=A_{pcq}\land B_p^{\psi}$ and $Z^{\psi}_{pcq}=Z_{pcq}\land B_p^{\psi}$ use the three-inequality linearization in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cycle-products-en}. Each active pair enters and exits once, ensuring uninterrupted occupancy. Total occupancy $v_p+B_p^{\psi}$ gives one production cycle to a $4\times1$ product pair and two adjacent production cycles in one chamber to a $2\times2$ product pair. Each $2\times2$ cycle contains at most one product pair receiving its first exposure and one receiving its last, forming an alternating exchange chain. An auxiliary-only support pair occupies one production cycle, and a cleaning pair occupies one cleaning cycle.

\noindent\textbf{Cycle durations and timing links.}
Let $S_{cq},E_{cq}$ be cycle start and end times, $d_m$ the duration of operation type $m$, and $\overline T$ a valid time horizon:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
E_{cq}-S_{cq}&=\sum_m d_mK_{cqm},\\
0\le S_{cq},E_{cq}&\le\overline T U_{cq},\\
S_{cq}&\ge E_{c,q-1}-M(1-U_{cq})\quad(q>1).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cycle-time-en}
\end{equation}
Cycle durations are fixed; waiting occurs between actions. Default durations are 180~s for $4\times1$ production, 90~s for $2\times2$ production, and 500~s for cleaning. Transfer and rotation durations are counted through their separate events.

\noindent\textbf{Front/back occupancy and rotating-chamber transfers.}
Binary $f_{pcq}$ selects the pair at the robot-accessible front side during a cycle. The derived binary indicators $\kappa_{cq},\upsilon_{cq}$ denote retention of one pair from the preceding cycle and complete departure after the current cycle. They follow from occupancy changes rather than independent choices:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\kappa_{cq}&=\sum_p(X_{pcq}-A_{pcq}),\\
\upsilon_{cq}&=U_{cq}-\sum_p(X_{pcq}-Z_{pcq}),\\
f_{pcq}&\le A_{pcq},\qquad\sum_pf_{pcq}=U_{cq},\\
f_{pc,q-1}&\ge X_{pcq}-A_{pcq}\quad(q>1),\\
f_{pcq}&\ge B_p+K_{cq\psi}+X_{pcq}-2-\kappa_{cq}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cycle-side-en}
\end{equation}
A newly loaded pair occupies the front side. A retained pair rotates to the back and must have occupied the front in the preceding cycle. At a new $2\times2$ chain head, the product pair is loaded last into the front. Pair loading and unloading times are $s_p^{\rm load},e_p^{\rm load},s_p^{\rm unload},e_p^{\rm unload}$. Rotation times before and after cycle $(c,q)$ are $s_{cq}^{\rm rot\pm},e_{cq}^{\rm rot\pm}$. These are aliases of physical events. Rotations $\mathrm{rot-}$ and $\mathrm{rot+}$ are activated by $U_{cq}$ and $\upsilon_{cq}$, respectively, and last $r_c$. Loading links are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
X_{pcq}=1&\Rightarrow S_{cq}\ge e^{\rm load}_p,\quad
s^{\rm unload}_p\ge E_{cq},\\
A_{pcq}=1,\ f_{pcq}=0&\Rightarrow e^{\rm load}_p\le s^{\rm rot-}_{cq},\\
A_{pcq}=1,\ f_{pcq}=1&\Rightarrow s^{\rm load}_p\ge e^{\rm rot-}_{cq},\quad
S_{cq}=e^{\rm load}_p,\\
\kappa_{cq}=1&\Rightarrow s^{\rm rot-}_{cq}\ge E_{c,q-1}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cycle-load-en}
\end{equation}
When both pairs leave, post-cycle rotation starts after the chamber operation ends. Unloading after the last exposure obeys
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
Z_{pcq}=1,\ f_{pcq}=1&\Rightarrow e^{\rm unload}_p\le s^{\rm rot+}_{cq},\\
Z_{pcq}=1,\ f_{pcq}=0,\ \upsilon_{cq}=1
&\Rightarrow s^{\rm unload}_p\ge e^{\rm rot+}_{cq},\\
Z_{pcq}=1,\ f_{pcq}=0,\ \upsilon_{cq}=0
&\Rightarrow s^{\rm unload}_p\ge e^{\rm rot-}_{c,q+1}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cycle-unload-en}
\end{equation}
If the next cycle retains a pair, its new pair waits until the replaced back pair has been unloaded; otherwise, the next loading waits for both previous pairs to leave. Indicator constraints or valid big-$M$ inequalities linearize these conditions, together with VTR exclusivity. Waiting remains explicit rather than being absorbed into rotation or transfer durations.

\noindent\textbf{Cleaning triggers and action chains.}
Integer $n_{cq}$ counts production cycles since the latest cleaning at completion of cycle $q$. With terminal candidate activation $U_{c,Q_{\max}+1}=0$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
0\le n_{cq}&\le H U_{cq},\qquad n_{c0}=0,\\
K_{cq,\mathrm{cl}}=1&\Rightarrow n_{cq}=0,\\
U_{cq}=1,\ K_{cq,\mathrm{cl}}=0
&\Rightarrow n_{cq}=n_{c,q-1}+1,\quad n_{c,q-1}\le H-1,\\
K_{cq,\mathrm{cl}}=1&\Rightarrow 1\le n_{c,q-1}\le H,\\
n_{cq}&\le H-1+H U_{c,q+1},\\
K_{c1,\mathrm{cl}}&=0,\qquad
K_{cq,\mathrm{cl}}+K_{c,q-1,\mathrm{cl}}\le1\quad(q>1).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:clean-counter-en}
\end{equation}
Each production cycle increments the count by one, and cleaning resets it to zero. Cleaning is mandatory at $H$. After 1 to $H-1$ production cycles, it may be selected at a valid boundary satisfying full occupancy, uninterrupted exposures, and resource constraints, with no restriction on the next production mode. Thus successive cleanings are separated by 1 to $H$ production cycles. A final segment below $H$ need not be followed by cleaning; reaching $H$ requires terminal cleaning. Equations~\eqref{eq:cycle-capacity-en} and \eqref{eq:cycle-exposures-en} keep the two exposures of each $2\times2$ product pair adjacent, so cleaning cannot split them.

Cleaning segments $e$ follow from cleaning boundaries in the cycle-type sequence. Let $\mathcal V_c$ contain production tasks, each being a $4\times1$ batch or a $2\times2$ production cycle. The indicator $a_{vce}$ assigns task $v$ to segment $e$ of chamber $c$, and $q^{\rm cl}_{ce}$ indicates cleaning after that segment. These indicators are linked to the cycle structure rather than providing independent cleaning-count or segmentation decisions. Internal segments end with cleaning; terminal cleaning follows Eq.~\eqref{eq:clean-counter-en}.

Task $v$ uses event times $s_v,e_v$. The complete cleaning task following segment $e$ has endpoints $s^{\rm cl}_{ce},e^{\rm cl}_{ce}$; its stage times are $s^{\rm cl,\nu}_{ce},e^{\rm cl,\nu}_{ce}$ for $\nu\in\{FL,BL,P,BU,FU\}$, denoting front loading, back loading, cleaning treatment, back unloading, and front unloading. These aliases refer to physical events; the complete task spans front-loading start through front-unloading completion. The timing relationships are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s_v&\ge e^{\rm cl}_{c,e-1}-M(1-a_{vce}) &&(e>1),\\
e_v&\le s^{\rm cl}_{ce}+M(1-a_{vce})+M(1-q^{\rm cl}_{ce}),\\
s^{\rm cl,BL}_{ce}&\ge e^{\rm cl,FL}_{ce}+r_c-M(1-q^{\rm cl}_{ce}),\\
s^{\rm cl,P}_{ce}&\ge e^{\rm cl,BL}_{ce}-M(1-q^{\rm cl}_{ce}),\\
s^{\rm cl,BU}_{ce}&\ge e^{\rm cl,P}_{ce}-M(1-q^{\rm cl}_{ce}),\\
s^{\rm cl,FU}_{ce}&\ge e^{\rm cl,BU}_{ce}+r_c-M(1-q^{\rm cl}_{ce}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:clean-time-en}
\end{equation}
Segment $e$ starts after the preceding cleaning task ends and ends before the next cleaning begins. A complete cleaning task includes loading auxiliary wafers on both sides, rotation, cleaning treatment, and unloading in reverse order. Its durations and resource occupancy constrain VTR, the chamber, and all four auxiliary wafers.

Once a solution is given, $4\times1$ batch sides, $2\times2$ product-pair chain positions, and cleaning segments can be recovered from $X,A,Z,f$ and cycle types without additional independent position variables. Fixed-layout experiments additionally fix these positions and service orders and permit filling only in the final short batch: if later same-type batches exist, the current $4\times1$ batch contains two product pairs. The joint model allows auxiliary filling in any valid production cycle.

\subsection{Auxiliary-wafer reuse and robot/module resources}
\label{sec:model-resource-en}

Wafer routes and chamber rules must also respect physical-resource availability. Each auxiliary loan is first assigned a physical identity, followed by physical-hand and module-slot assignments. Resource orders and residency limits coordinate their occupancy.

\noindent\textbf{Auxiliary identities and reuse.}
Each auxiliary member position defines a loan request $j=(p,r)$, activated directly by $d_{pr}$ for product--auxiliary, auxiliary-only filling, or cleaning pairs. Membership and cycle choices link its supply and return events. Physical auxiliary occupancy extends from storage pickup through completed return.

All chambers access one physical auxiliary inventory. The set $\mathcal T_c$ contains identities accessible to chamber $c$; loans of the same identity must be exclusive across chambers as well. Each active request selects one physical wafer:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal T_c}g_{j\tau}=d_{pr},
\qquad j=(p,r)\in\mathcal J_c^D.
\label{eq:dummy-assign-en}
\end{equation}
The assignment $g_{j\tau}$ also marks occupancy of the borrowed physical identity. Shared-resource constraints order successive loans, permitting reuse only after unloading and completed return to storage. Identity choices determine which cross-chamber loans compete: even with identical product pairing, chamber assignment, and cycle structure, different identities can change concurrent production intervals. Section~\ref{sec:joint-evidence-en} quantifies this effect with product structure controlled.

\noindent\textbf{Robot hands and module capacities.}
ATR has two hands, VTR has four, and each rotating chamber has four wafer positions. At any instant, each ATR or VTR executes at most one coordinated transfer, and each chamber executes at most one unified loading, unloading, rotation, or chamber-operation stage. Their actuators are therefore exclusive action resources. Individual hand assignments express robot carrying capacity, and pair occupancy expresses chamber capacity; actuator exclusivity does not represent wafer count. AL is also exclusive. The upper and lower load locks contain slots U1/U2 and L1/L2, respectively, and every physical auxiliary wafer is a reusable identity. Each occupancy interval selects one allowed unit, whether a physical hand, an auxiliary identity, or an exclusive actuator. For occupancy task $o$,
\begin{equation}
\sum_{\ell\in\Gamma_o}h_{o\ell}=b_o.
\label{eq:slot-select-en}
\end{equation}
Binary $b_o$ activates the task, $h_{o\ell}$ selects unit $\ell$, and $\Gamma_o$ is the set of allowed units. Inactive tasks select no unit. Synchronized wafers retain separate hand occupancies, with synchronization enforced by Eq.~\eqref{eq:double-transfer-en}. Full chamber occupancy follows from pair assignments. Equation~\eqref{eq:resource-order-en} prevents overlapping occupancy of any physical unit. If $\Gamma_o$ is a singleton, $h_{o\ell}=b_o$ can be substituted directly. Auxiliary-identity occupancy uses $g_{j\tau}$ without a duplicate assignment decision.

Upper-slot occupancy extends from ATR placement start through VTR pickup completion plus reset time. Lower-slot occupancy extends from VTR placement start through ATR pickup completion plus reset time. Reset limits reuse of that slot and may overlap subsequent robot travel.

VTR has dirty hands $D_1,D_2$ and clean hands $C_1,C_2$. Product holding from upper-lock pickup to chamber placement permits $\Gamma_o=\{D_1,D_2\}$; holding after all required exposures, from chamber pickup to lower-lock placement, permits $\Gamma_o=\{C_1,C_2\}$. Auxiliary input and return permit all four hands. Each holding interval lasts from pickup start through placement completion and retains the same physical hand selected by Eq.~\eqref{eq:slot-select-en}. Same-hand intervals are exclusive with zero hand-switching gap; robot travel is separately included through actuator constraints. Each hand class therefore holds at most two wafers, with auxiliary wafers competing for both classes. A $2\times2$ product remains inside the chamber between its two exposures and leaves on a clean hand after the second. Paired exchange permits dirty hands to hold an incoming pair while clean hands remove a completed pair, provided every auxiliary wafer is also assigned a free physical hand.

\noindent\textbf{Resource exclusivity and location-dependent travel.}
Resource orders are either prescribed or selected. Precedence arcs and predefined service chains impose the former; order variables resolve other conflicts. Fixed-layout pure-$4\times1$ experiments prescribe aligner and load-lock service orders and, when the number of candidate batches does not exceed the cleaning threshold, a VTR batch-transfer chain. These restrictions apply only to fixed-layout experiments; the joint model chooses the remaining resource orders.

For tasks $o,o'$ competing for unit $\ell$ of resource $r$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s_{o'}&\ge e_o+\rho^r_{oo'}-M(1-y^r_{oo'})
-M(1-h_{o\ell})-M(1-h_{o'\ell}),\\
s_o&\ge e_{o'}+\rho^r_{o'o}-My^r_{oo'}
-M(1-h_{o\ell})-M(1-h_{o'\ell}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:resource-order-en}
\end{equation}
When both tasks select the unit, binary $y^r_{oo'}$ chooses their order. The parameter $\rho^r_{oo'}$ includes repositioning or reset time. The constraint covers ATR, AL, load locks, VTR, chambers, and physical auxiliary identities, preventing conflicting actuator actions as well as premature reuse of slots or wafers.

Travel times are specified by location pairs. For actions whose chamber is chosen by $z_{pc}$, the relevant travel gap is activated by the selected chamber combination rather than a predetermined location. For example, when active exclusive chamber actions $o,o'$ serve pairs $p,p'$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
z_{pc}=z_{p'c'}=1,\ y^{\rm VTR}_{oo'}=1
\ \Rightarrow\ s_{o'}\ge e_o+\rho^{\rm VTR}_{cc'}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:location-travel-en}
\end{equation}
The parameter $\rho^{\rm VTR}_{cc'}$ is the travel time from chamber $c$ to $c'$. Reverse order is handled symmetrically; fixed-location actions use Eq.~\eqref{eq:resource-order-en} directly.

\noindent\textbf{Residency limits and timing feasibility.}
For the events at which wafer $w$ enters and leaves constrained equipment region $r$,
\begin{equation}
0\le s_{\operatorname{out}(w,r)}-e_{\operatorname{in}(w,r)}
\le\overline R_{wr}.
\label{eq:residence-en}
\end{equation}
This bounds physical residency in a load lock, chamber, or constrained transport segment. Load-lock slot occupancy ends after the wafer is picked up from the opposite side; subsequent pressure reset may overlap robot travel, while the next incompatible load-lock action must await reset completion.

Residency limits enforce temporal feasibility in the corresponding segments; they do not require same-recipe wafers to have identical residency durations. Necessary processing and handling times can also differ by mode and segment. A study of residency uniformity would need a dispersion measure for comparable wafers with the same recipe and segment, evaluated or optimized directly. Reduced waiting is not a substitute for that test.

The precedence constraints in Eq.~\eqref{eq:precedence-en} and resource-order constraints in Eq.~\eqref{eq:resource-order-en} jointly prevent modeled deadlocks. Construct a directed graph whose nodes are active actions and whose arcs are technical precedence or selected resource orders. A circular wait among robots, load locks, chambers, or auxiliary wafers creates a directed cycle. Summing its time inequalities accumulates a positive duration or switching increment for actual occupancy actions, requiring an action to start strictly later than itself. Such an order is infeasible. Together with explicit load-lock slots, full chamber occupancy, and return-before-reuse requirements, time feasibility excludes circular waiting among the modeled actions and permits wafers to complete their routes.

\subsection{Makespan-first two-stage optimization}
\label{sec:model-objective-en}

The preceding constraints define the joint feasible region $\mathcal F$, with $\xi$ the vector of retained variables. Stage one optimizes product-return makespan. Stage two improves waiting and production cadence under the first-stage makespan bound and principal discrete structure.

\noindent\textbf{Stage one: minimize product-return makespan.}
The return-action end time $e_{w,\mathrm{return}}$ directly denotes product $w$'s return to its original LP:
\begin{equation}
C_{\max}\ge e_{w,\mathrm{return}}\ (w\in\mathcal W),\qquad
\min_{\xi\in\mathcal F}C_{\max}.
\label{eq:primary-objective-en}
\end{equation}
Makespan is measured by the last product's completion of its entire route. Cleaning between production segments delays subsequent products through resource and precedence constraints and thus affects makespan. Terminal cleaning after the last production remains in the action sequence but its completion is not a direct lower bound on $C_{\max}$. It may still delay returns from another chamber through the shared VTR, so indirect effects require the complete schedule. WPH uses product-return $C_{\max}$ as its denominator. The joint feasible region includes pairing, chamber assignment, cycle occupancy, and complete physical timing; fixed-layout experiments add their layout and service-order restrictions.

\noindent\textbf{Stage two: restrict the refinement domain.}
Let $\widehat\xi$ be the best first-stage incumbent and $\widehat C$ its product makespan. The symbol $\xi_d$ denotes component $d$, and hatted quantities are constants in stage two. The index set $\mathcal D_{\rm fix}$ covers fixed pairing, chamber assignments, cycle occupancy, auxiliary identities, hand assignments, accompanying-entry choices, and resource orders. With nonnegative makespan tolerance $\varepsilon_C$, impose
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\xi_d=\widehat\xi_d\quad(d\in\mathcal D_{\rm fix}),\qquad
C_{\max}\le\widehat C+\varepsilon_C.
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:secondary-domain-en}
\end{equation}
These constraints retain principal discrete structure and the makespan bound, while same-location atomic-transfer synchronization and continuous times remain adjustable. Stage two can start from a first-stage incumbent even without a proof of optimality.

Let $\mathcal Q^w$ index the waiting items and $\omega_\alpha$ be the nonnegative interval remaining after subtracting necessary action or travel time between the corresponding events. Total waiting enters the objective directly as $\sum_\alpha\omega_\alpha$. The experimental label $L_{\rm sum}$ is only a summary statistic for this sum, not an independent variable. The quantity $L_{\max}$ bounds the largest individual wait. Simultaneous waits of different wafers are counted separately. Each $\omega_\alpha$ is an event-time difference expression rather than an independent waiting decision:
\begin{equation}
L_{\max}\ge\omega_\alpha\ge0,\qquad \alpha\in\mathcal Q^w.
\label{eq:secondary-wait-en}
\end{equation}

Cadence uses cycle start times $S_{cq}$ directly. The set $\mathcal I_h$ contains the central indices $(c,q)$ of three consecutive production cycles within the same chamber and $2\times2$ chain. Both boundaries retain a pair, so $\kappa_{cq}=\kappa_{c,q+1}=1$; differences do not cross cleaning or chain boundaries. Fixing cycle structure in stage two fixes this set. The difference between successive start-to-start intervals is $S_{c,q+1}-2S_{cq}+S_{c,q-1}$. Nonnegative $v_{cq}$ bounds its absolute value linearly without a separate segment clock or difference variable:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
v_{cq}&\ge S_{c,q+1}-2S_{cq}+S_{c,q-1},\\
v_{cq}&\ge-S_{c,q+1}+2S_{cq}-S_{c,q-1}
\quad((c,q)\in\mathcal I_h).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:secondary-rhythm-en}
\end{equation}

When soft waiting limits $\bar\omega_\alpha$ are enabled, nonnegative $\eta_\alpha$ bounds excess waiting:
\begin{equation}
\eta_\alpha\ge\omega_\alpha-\bar\omega_\alpha,\qquad
\eta_\alpha\ge0.
\label{eq:secondary-softwait-en}
\end{equation}

With nonnegative weights $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_4$, the second-stage objective is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min\ \ &\lambda_1\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal Q^w}\omega_\alpha+\lambda_2L_{\max}\\
&+\lambda_3\sum_{(c,q)\in\mathcal I_h}v_{cq}
+\lambda_4\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal Q^w}\eta_\alpha.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:secondary-objective-en}
\end{equation}
The terms penalize total waiting, maximum individual waiting, variation in adjacent production-cycle start intervals, and soft-limit excess. The experiments use $(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3)=(1,10,25)$ and $\varepsilon_C=10^{-6}$. Disabling soft waiting limits omits their variables and penalty.

At an optimal second-stage solution with strictly positive corresponding weights, the auxiliary bounds are tight: $L_{\max}=\max_\alpha\omega_\alpha$, $v_{cq}=|S_{c,q+1}-2S_{cq}+S_{c,q-1}|$, and $\eta_\alpha=\max(0,\omega_\alpha-\bar\omega_\alpha)$. Time-limited feasible solutions may retain slack, so reported metrics are recomputed from actual event times. Cadence variation is zero for three equally spaced production starts. Soft waiting limits allow penalized excess; residency limits remain hard constraints.


\paragraph{Waiting, cadence, and JIT.} Equation~\eqref{eq:secondary-wait-en} reduces event intervals after subtracting necessary durations, whereas Eq.~\eqref{eq:secondary-rhythm-en} reduces changes in consecutive cycle-start intervals within one chain. Neither directly minimizes differences in wafer residency. Even equally spaced cycle starts can yield different residency durations through unequal unloading waits. Reducing waiting and irregular coordination supports JIT-oriented operation, but the model does not include demand-matching targets, earliness/tardiness costs, or a residency-uniformity objective.

\section{Solution framework for time-limited feasible scheduling}


The algorithmic task is to return a schedule satisfying the complete physical constraints within specified time and memory budgets, then improve its objective and optimality bounds. Updated equipment parameters can define a new deterministic instance for this workflow; an old schedule is not assumed to remain feasible under new parameters. Limited computation reflects both the complexity of joint scheduling and the need for timely decisions after updates, although no real equipment rescheduling deadline is measured here. SPBS targets feasible initialization, timing refinement improves the selected structure, and learned cut selection is an experimentally assessed search component rather than a prerequisite for mode-selection conclusions.

Section 2 links membership and cycle occupancy to complete wafer routes in a joint model. The solution framework combines structured initialization, MILP search, and timing refinement for both the fixed-configuration special case and the joint model. The fixed-layout benchmark supports training and component comparisons; the joint model evaluates search with the frozen learned policy. Primal feasibility requires coordination of full occupancy, dual-source logistics, and shared-resource order, whereas dual-bound improvement depends on valid inequalities acting on discrete choices, continuous times, and their coupling. We exploit these structures through a structure-preserving binary skeleton (SPBS) for initial feasible scheduling and \method{} for cut selection. SPBS supplies a feasible starting point, and \method{} determines cut counts and insertion order. SCIP generates valid cuts, checks feasibility, maintains bounds, and certifies optimality \citep{achterberg2009scip,bestuzheva2023scip}.

The solution process comprises initial-schedule construction and validation, candidate-cut generation, count prediction, ordered-subset search, and role-submodular completion, followed by continued branch-and-bound search. All selected cuts are valid inequalities for the original model and retain their coefficients. Cut selection therefore controls the strengthening of the linear relaxation while preserving the original integer feasible set.

\subsection{SPBS initial solution}

For the evaluated fixed-configuration special case, SPBS retains its pairing, chamber and cleaning layout, constructs a skeleton for selectable resource orders and slots, and then solves for event times in a restricted MILP. Product wafers are paired in input order. The $4\times1$ pairs are mapped sequentially to chambers, batches, and front/back positions, whereas the $2\times2$ pairs are assigned round-robin to chamber-chain positions. This determines batch-use, tail-batch, used-position, and last-position indicators. Cleaning counts partition each chamber sequence into process segments: $4\times1$ batches are assigned to their cleaning epochs, while a $2\times2$ chain ends its current segment with auxiliary wafers when the cleaning threshold is reached and resumes after cleaning with a new auxiliary-bounded head. Processing assignments, chamber membership, and cleaning boundaries follow from the benchmark configuration; SPBS coordinates auxiliary occupancy and event times within that layout.

At the resource level, SPBS derives a conservative order from the same physical route. ATR first schedules $4\times1$ inputs and returns across chambers and stages the first $2\times2$ pair of a chamber immediately after that chamber's final full batch. It then advances chain inputs and returns segment by segment. Deterministic odd/even load-lock slots break slot symmetry, and the VTR order is aligned with ATR batch progression and cleaning actions. Let $\mathcal F_q$ be the binary variables fixed in relaxation level $q$, with skeleton value $\widehat x_j$. The restricted feasibility subproblem imposes
\begin{equation}
\ell_j^{(q)}=u_j^{(q)}=\widehat x_j,\qquad j\in\mathcal F_q
\label{eq:spbs-fix-en}
\end{equation}

Here $j$ indexes scalar MILP variable $x_j$, and $q\in\{1,\ldots,5\}$ indexes the initialization relaxation level. The bounds $\ell_j^{(q)},u_j^{(q)}$ apply to $x_j$ at that level, and $\widehat x_j$ is its skeleton value. The fixed-index set $\mathcal F_q$ differs from feasible region $\mathcal F$ in Section 2; $q$ here is not a chamber-cycle index.

Equation~\eqref{eq:spbs-fix-en} fixes only the skeleton choices at the current level. Start, completion, and waiting times, together with resource orders not fixed at that level, remain free.

SPBS progressively relaxes fixed-variable conditions through five levels, with nested fixed sets $\mathcal F_1\supseteq\mathcal F_2\supseteq\cdots\supseteq\mathcal F_5$. For unchanged skeleton values, releasing these conditions expands the restricted feasible set. If a level yields no feasible solution within its allocated budget, the search proceeds to the next level. The first level fixes product and batch assignments, activation states, cleaning segments, load-lock slots, and ATR, AL, LL, and VTR orders. The second releases VTR order; the third also releases load-lock order; the fourth releases all remaining resource orders while retaining structural assignments and slots; and the fifth retains only assignments, activation states, and cleaning segments. For instances with at least 32 wafers, the search starts at the fourth or fifth level. Under a tight waiting-time limit, the original feasible set without additional skeleton restrictions is also considered.

Each level solves a restricted feasibility subproblem with a zero objective and terminates at the first feasible solution. These subproblems use feasibility emphasis, aggressive heuristics, and fast presolve, without cut separation. The resulting principal discrete decisions and continuous event times are then retained in the full model while auxiliary variables associated with schedule stability are completed. A schedule satisfying all original-model constraints becomes the initial incumbent of the main problem. The main problem restores the bounds temporarily tightened by SPBS while retaining the original layout equalities; its feasible set is the complete set conditional on the prescribed layout. In the experiments, SPBS schedules are constructed offline and checked against their corresponding models; the PDI integration interval and main-solve timing both exclude offline initial-schedule construction.

Hierarchical initialization for the joint model starts from a schedule satisfying complete wafer routes and physical-hand eligibility and improves the skeleton with product makespan as its objective. When enabled, its five levels retain the principal discrete choices, release VTR action orders, release load-lock orders, release the remaining resource orders, and finally retain only structural choices such as pairing, cycle activation, and cycle type. Paired-action synchronization and continuous times remain adjustable at every level. Each level receives at most one fifth of the initialization budget and passes its best complete feasible assignment to the next level. All temporary restrictions are removed before joint main search, which again optimizes pairing, chamber assignment, and resource order. This makespan-improvement initialization differs from the zero-objective feasibility search used for fixed-layout SPBS; construction and main-solve budgets are accounted for separately.

\subsection{HEM baseline and role-aware representation}

Prior learning-based cut selectors address generic candidate sets with reinforcement learning, learned ranking, and look-ahead imitation \citep{tang2020cut,huang2022cutranking,paulus2022looking}. HEM further identifies three coupled decisions---which cuts, how many cuts, and in what order---and handles them with a high-level count policy and a low-level sequence policy \citep{wang2023hem}. We retain HEM's hierarchical decomposition and pointer decoding and use its 13D sequence model as the direct baseline. Training instances, budgets, random seeds, candidate limits, and hidden dimensions are shared while representation, decoding, and post-selection completion are compared. HEM was subsequently extended to hierarchical sequence/set models \citep{wang2024hemset}; HEM-13D here is a controlled reproduction of the 2023 sequence model.

The 13D HEM candidate vector comprises objective parallelism, efficacy, normalized support, integral-support ratio, normalized violation, the mean/maximum/minimum/standard deviation of row coefficients, and the corresponding four objective-coefficient statistics. These features describe immediate LP quality and numerical scale. They do not distinguish two similarly efficacious rows when one acts on discrete route choices and the other on continuous event times. In the present model, that distinction corresponds directly to changing a process/resource combination versus tightening the timing of an existing combination.

Starting from the 13 generic HEM features, variables are partitioned by their mathematical attributes into binary, general integer, objective-active continuous, continuous auxiliary, and other roles. The coefficient mass of role $g$ in cut $i$ is
\begin{equation}
p_{ig}=\frac{\sum_{j:x_j\in g}|a_{ij}|}{\sum_j|a_{ij}|+\varepsilon}.
\label{eq:role-mass-en}
\end{equation}

In Eq.~\eqref{eq:role-mass-en}, $i$ indexes candidate cuts and $j$ indexes original scalar MILP variables $x_j$; $a_{ij}$ is the coefficient of $x_j$ in cut $i$. The index $g\in\mathcal G$ denotes a mathematical variable role, $p_{ig}$ its absolute coefficient-mass fraction in cut $i$, and $\varepsilon>0$ a denominator-stabilization constant. The five roles are binary, general integer, objective-active continuous, continuous auxiliary, and other; $B,I,C_{\rm obj},C_{\rm aux}$ label the first four. The cut coefficient $a_{ij}$ differs from wafer-membership indicator $a_{wpr}$ in Section 2.

Equation~\eqref{eq:role-mass-en} is the fraction of absolute cut-coefficient mass carried by a mathematical variable role. Beyond the five role masses, the added features are mass on variables with two finite global bounds, positive-coefficient mass, discrete--continuous coupling $4(p_{iB}+p_{iI})(p_{iC_{\rm obj}}+p_{iC_{\rm aux}})$, the largest non-residual role mass, and normalized role entropy. The resulting vector has 23 dimensions. Role assignment depends only on variable types, objective coefficients, and global bounds. The representation is therefore invariant to variable renaming that preserves these attributes and the cut coefficients.

HEM uses a hierarchical ``count policy + ordered-subset policy'' decomposition: its high level predicts the number of cuts from a sequence representation, while its low level uses an LSTM encoder--decoder with pointer attention to select cuts sequentially \citep{wang2023hem,vinyals2015pointer}. Building on this hierarchy, the proposed selector introduces a role-aware grouped gated encoder before the LSTM, a role-submodular completion step after the pointer sequence, and width-three beam search during formal inference, which together form the complete RDMCT-HBS selection pipeline.


\subsection{Role-aware encoding and hierarchical sequence decoding}

Let $m_t$ be the number of candidates retained at separation round $t$. For candidate cut $i$, write the 23D input as $x_i=[x_i^{H};x_i^{R}]$, where $x_i^{H}\in\mathbb R^{13}$ contains the generic HEM features and $x_i^{R}\in\mathbb R^{10}$ contains the proposed role-aware features. The full vector is first layer-normalized; the two feature groups are then projected separately into the common hidden space and fused by an elementwise gate generated from all 23 features:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde x_i&=\operatorname{LN}(x_i),\\
u_i^{H}&=W_H\widetilde x_i^{H}+b_H,\qquad
u_i^{R}=W_R\widetilde x_i^{R}+b_R,\\
g_i&=\sigma(W_g\widetilde x_i+b_g),\qquad
z_i=g_i\odot u_i^{H}+(1-g_i)\odot u_i^{R}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:gated-encoding-en}
\end{equation}

The index $i$ identifies a candidate cut. Superscripts $H,R$ label the generic HEM and role-feature branches. The operator $\operatorname{LN}$ produces the layer-normalized vector $\widetilde x_i$, with components $\widetilde x_i^H,\widetilde x_i^R$. The projected vectors $u_i^H,u_i^R$, gate $g_i$, and fused vector $z_i$ have 128 components. Matrices $W_H,W_R,W_g$ and biases $b_H,b_R,b_g$ parameterize the two projections and gate; their subscripts identify network components. The function $\sigma(\cdot)$ is sigmoid and $\odot$ is elementwise multiplication. Weights and biases are learned during training and fixed during inference. This $\sigma$ is unrelated to the source-configuration input in Section 2.

Here $z_i\in\mathbb R^{128}$ is the fused candidate embedding. Unlike a single projection of the 23D vector, the two branches preserve generic LP quality and mathematical-role structure separately, while $g_i$ determines their candidate-specific contribution in each hidden dimension. Thus two cuts with similar efficacy but different concentration on discrete route variables or continuous timing variables can remain distinguishable. This grouped gated adapter is a structural change to HEM's input representation introduced in this work.

The fused candidates, in the deterministic order established by Step~1, are passed through a single-layer LSTM encoder:
\begin{equation}
(h_i,c_i)=\operatorname{LSTM}(z_i,h_{i-1},c_{i-1}),\qquad i=1,\ldots,m_t .
\label{eq:lstm-encoding-en}
\end{equation}

The index $t$ denotes a separation round, $m_t$ its candidate count after truncation, and $i=1,\ldots,m_t$ the encoding order. Vectors $h_i,c_i$ are the hidden and memory states after candidate $i$, and $h_{i-1},c_{i-1}$ are the preceding states; both have 128 components. The memory state $c_i$ is not chamber index $c$.

The states $h_i$ turn local cut descriptors into context-dependent candidate representations and are used by both the high-level retained-count policy and the low-level ordered selector. A 128D hidden state is used to encode the ordered candidates into a contextual sequence representation. Fixing the input order also makes the encoded sequence reproducible for an identical candidate pool.

At low-level decoding step $r$, the decoder state $d_r$ formed from the selected prefix and all encoder states $h_i$ are used for one glimpse attention followed by pointer attention. Let $M_{ri}=-\infty$ if candidate $i$ already appears in the prefix and $M_{ri}=0$ otherwise. Then
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
a_{ri}&=v_g^\top\tanh(W_e h_i+W_d d_r)+M_{ri},\\
\alpha_{ri}&=\operatorname{softmax}_i(a_{ri}),\\
\bar h_r&=\sum_i\alpha_{ri}h_i,\\
b_{ri}&=v_p^\top\tanh(U_e h_i+U_g\bar h_r)+M_{ri},\\
P(i_r=i\mid i_{1:r-1},S_t)&=\operatorname{softmax}_i(b_{ri}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:pointer-decoding-en}
\end{equation}

Subscripts $r,i$ denote decoding step and candidate cut. The quantities $a_{ri},\alpha_{ri},\bar h_r,b_{ri}$ are the glimpse score, its candidate-normalized attention weight, the weighted candidate summary, and the pointer score. Matrices $W_e,W_d$ project encoder and decoder states; $U_e,U_g$ project encoder states and the glimpse summary. Vectors $v_g,v_p$ are trainable scoring vectors; subscripts e, d, g, and p label network components. The candidate mask $M_{ri}$ differs from time constant $M$. The selected cut at step $r$ is $i_r$, its preceding ordered prefix is $i_{1:r-1}$, and $S_t$ is the encoded candidate state for round $t$. The notation $P(\cdot\mid\cdot)$ denotes a conditional selection probability.

Equation~\eqref{eq:pointer-decoding-en} makes the sequential dependence explicit. The selected prefix updates the decoder state and the next conditional distribution. Masking excludes previously selected cuts, and the glimpse summarizes the candidate pool for the current prefix. The resulting sequence model combines contextual candidate encoding, prefix-aware pointer decoding, and the proposed representation and completion stages into one role-aware selector.

Training keeps stochastic sampling from the policy for exploration. During formal inference, instead of committing to one path at every step, the method maintains $B=3$ partial sequences. For parent beam $b=(i_1,\ldots,i_{r-1})$ and an unselected extension $j$, the accumulated score is
\begin{equation}
L_r(b\oplus j)=L_{r-1}(b)+\log P(i_r=j\mid b,S_t),
\label{eq:beam-score-en}
\end{equation}

Here $b$ denotes a parent prefix and $j$ an unselected candidate cut; $b\oplus j$ appends $j$. The score $L_r$ is joint log probability for a length-$r$ prefix, with $L_0=0$ for the empty prefix. The symbols $B$ and $\mathcal B_r$ denote beam width and retained prefixes at step $r$, not production batches.

All parent-beam--candidate extensions are compared and the three largest $L_r$ values are retained. After the high-level target length $k_t$ is reached, the best sequence is passed to the completion stage. This beam procedure preserves multiple high-probability partial sequences during decoding, reduces early commitment, and works jointly with the subsequent policy-anchored role-submodular completion to inject variable-role structure into the final selected set.

\subsection{Stepwise selection from candidate pool to ordered cuts}

At separation round $t$, the method executes the following sequence.

\noindent\textbf{Step 1: form the candidate pool.} SCIP first generates and validates candidate rows $\mathcal C_t$ with its own separators. To control the candidate-state dimension and evaluation cost, at most 128 candidates are retained in descending order of cut efficacy. Ties are broken by original index for deterministic input. Forced cuts remain under solver control and are not placed in the learned ranking.

\noindent\textbf{Step 2: compute and encode the state.} The 13 HEM and 10 role-aware features are extracted for every row, together with role masses, dominant role, and structural entropy. Equation~\eqref{eq:gated-encoding-en} then performs grouped gated fusion and Eq.~\eqref{eq:lstm-encoding-en} produces contextual candidate states. The encoded candidate set is $S_t$.

\noindent\textbf{Step 3: determine the retained count.} The high-level policy outputs a tanh-bounded action in $[-1,1]$, which is mapped to a retained fraction $\rho_t$. The fraction is then clipped by the global 0.2 cap, SCIP's round-specific limit, and the limit of 16 cuts, producing $k_t$. When enough candidates exist, at least two rows are retained to maintain a nonempty selected subset in each round.

\noindent\textbf{Step 4: construct the candidate order.} At low-level step $r$, the pointer policy conditions on $S_t$ and the selected prefix $i_{1:r-1}$ to score all remaining candidates. Selected indices are masked with negative infinity and cannot be repeated. The joint high- and low-level policy is
\begin{equation}
\pi_\theta(k_t,i_{1:k_t}\mid S_t)=\pi_\theta^H(k_t\mid S_t)
\prod_{r=1}^{k_t}\pi_\theta^L(i_r\mid S_t,i_{1:r-1}).
\label{eq:policy-en}
\end{equation}

The parameter vector is $\theta$, the retained count at round $t$ is $k_t$, and $i_{1:k_t}$ is the ordered cut sequence. The policies $\pi_\theta^H,\pi_\theta^L$ are the high-level count and low-level conditional selection policies; superscripts H and L denote their levels. The retained fraction $\rho_t$ is mapped from the high-level output; subscript $t$ denotes the separation round.

Equation~\eqref{eq:policy-en} means that the high level determines the sequence length and the low level then chooses distinct candidates within that length. Each earlier cut changes both the remaining action set and the decoder state used by later probabilities.

\noindent\textbf{Step 5: beam decoding.} The low-level policy samples stochastically during training. At test time, beam search of width three is used. At step $r$, every partial sequence is extended by every unselected candidate; joint log-probabilities are accumulated, and the three highest-scoring items over all parent-beam--next-candidate pairs are retained. After $k_t$ steps, the best sequence is returned. Unlike one-step greedy decoding, the beam retains an initially lower-ranked prefix whose later joint probability can be higher. This fixed-width neural construction follows the broader use of beam decoding in neural combinatorial optimization \citep{choo2022beam}.

\noindent\textbf{Step 6: role-submodular completion.} A sequence based only on joint probability can still repeatedly cover one variable role. The first $\lceil0.25k_t\rceil$ pointer candidates are retained as policy anchors. The remaining policy order is merged with a heuristic tail sorted by instantaneous structural quality $q_i$, and at most $2k_t$ rows form expansion pool $\mathcal R_t$. For $S\subseteq\mathcal R_t$, define
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
F_t(S)={}&\sum_{i\in S}(0.35q_i+0.20r_i)
+0.25\sum_{g\in\mathcal G}d_g\sqrt{\sum_{i\in S}p_{ig}}\\
&+\frac{0.20}{|\mathcal R_t|}\sum_{u\in\mathcal R_t}\max_{i\in S}s_{ui},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:submodular-en}
\end{equation}

In Eq.~\eqref{eq:submodular-en}, $t$ denotes a separation round, $S$ the selected subset of expansion pool $\mathcal R_t$, $i$ a selected cut, $u$ a represented pool candidate, and $g$ a variable role. The quantities $q_i,r_i,d_g,s_{ui}$ denote structural quality, pointer-rank prior, role demand share, and clipped candidate similarity. The function $F_t(S)$ is surrogate set utility. The set $A_t$ contains fixed policy anchors, $U\subseteq\mathcal R_t\setminus A_t$ is a completion subset, and $f(U)$ measures utility added to the anchors. These $q_i,r_i,d_g$ are cut-selection quantities, not chamber cycles, resources, or action durations.

where $r_i$ is the normalized pointer-rank prior, $d_g$ is the demand share of role $g$ in the expansion pool, and $s_{ui}$ is clipped cosine similarity on generic-quality and role-mass descriptors. The score $q_i$ combines efficacy, violation, integral support, recognized-role ratio, dominant-role ratio, and structural entropy. In Eq.~\eqref{eq:submodular-en}, the first term retains instantaneous quality and the learned order; the square-root coverage term gives diminishing marginal gain to repeated role mass; and the final term makes the selected cuts represent the main structures in the pool. Starting from the anchors, the method recomputes the marginal gain of every unselected candidate and adds the largest until $k_t$ cuts have been selected. With nonnegative weights, role masses, and clipped similarities, and $F_t(\varnothing)=0$, the modular, concave-coverage, and facility-location terms form a monotone submodular objective. For fixed anchors $A_t$ and expansion pool, define $f(U)=F_t(A_t\cup U)-F_t(A_t)$. Greedy completion under the cardinality constraint has the standard $1-1/e$ approximation property for this residual objective \citep{nemhauser1978analysis}. This ratio concerns coverage under the residual surrogate objective within the fixed pool; PDI and solution time are evaluated experimentally.

The final ordered sequence is denoted $S_t^\star$, with $\star$ labeling the output of this round. The $k_t$ selected cuts are added to the current LP in anchor order followed by greedy-completion order. Candidate truncation, state encoding, count prediction, sequence decoding, and submodular completion jointly determine the ordered cut set used to strengthen the relaxation in the current round.

\textbf{5. Initialize the beam.} Set $\mathcal B_0$ to the empty sequence with log probability zero.\\
\textbf{6. Expand.} For $r=1,\ldots,k_t$, obtain masked conditional probabilities from one glimpse plus pointer attention, extend every prefix in $\mathcal B_{r-1}$ by every unselected candidate, and accumulate log probability.\\
\textbf{7. Prune.} Retain the three highest joint-probability parent--candidate extensions as $\mathcal B_r$; after the loop, take the leading sequence in $\mathcal B_{k_t}$.\\
\textbf{8. Fix anchors.} Retain its first $\lceil0.25k_t\rceil$ rows as policy anchors $A_t$.\\
\textbf{9. Form the expansion pool.} Merge the remaining pointer order with structural-quality order, remove duplicates, and keep at most $2k_t$ rows as $\mathcal R_t$.\\
\textbf{10. Complete submodularly.} Set $S\leftarrow A_t$; while $|S|<k_t$, add the $i\in\mathcal R_t\setminus S$ maximizing $F_t(S\cup\{i\})-F_t(S)$.\\
\textbf{11. Add cuts.} Add $S_t^\star$ to the current LP in anchor order followed by completion order.\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Policy training and exact solution}

One time-limited SCIP solve forms a rollout, with its terminal PDI used as the cost. The current policy is executed in every eligible cut-selection round. Training uses the high-level fraction and low-level sampled sequence at the first valid candidate set of that rollout as its action sample, and the PDI of the complete solve is returned to both policies. Writing $R=-\pdi$ as a reward, the low-level estimator is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{\nabla_\theta J}
&=\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B(R_b-\bar R_{\rm EMA})
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(\tau_b),\\
\bar R_{\rm EMA}&\leftarrow0.9\bar R_{\rm EMA}+0.1\bar R.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:reinforce-en}
\end{equation}

The objective $J$ is expected return, $\nabla_\theta J$ its parameter gradient, and a hat denotes a sample estimate. Here $B$ is the rollout batch size and $b=1,\ldots,B$ a rollout index. The return is $R_b$, $\tau_b$ is the cut sequence sampled at the first eligible candidate set of rollout $b$, and $\pi_\theta(\tau_b)$ its policy probability. The batch-mean return is $\bar R$ and the exponential-moving-average baseline is $\bar R_{\rm EMA}$, with EMA a method label. These uses of $B,b,\tau_b$ differ from beam width, parent prefixes, and physical auxiliary identities.

Equation~\eqref{eq:reinforce-en} gives the batch policy-gradient update formed from the first eligible candidate-set action sample and an exponential-moving-average baseline; $B$ is the rollout batch and $\tau_b$ is the cut sequence sampled at the first eligible candidate set in rollout $b$. The equivalent loss is PDI-cost advantage times log probability. Low-level log probability is averaged over sequence length so that a longer cut sequence does not produce a larger gradient merely because it contains more terms. The high-level policy uses the same PDI cost and an independent EMA baseline and is updated every two epochs; both gradient norms are clipped at 2. Parallel environments collect rollouts, and a central learner updates both policies synchronously after each batch, forming a parallel hierarchical REINFORCE procedure \citep{williams1992reinforce,mnih2016a3c}.

Training uses five instances and five random seeds. Each seed has two workers, each completing four 30-s/1024-MB rollouts per epoch, for eight rollouts per epoch and 60 epochs. Initial Adam learning rates are $10^{-4}$ for the low-level policy and $5\times10^{-4}$ for the high-level policy and are multiplied by 0.96 every ten epochs. Training uses stochastic decoding and formal inference uses beam width three. Every configuration--seed combination uses the fixed epoch-60 parameters without benchmark- or test-set parameter selection. HEM-13D, feature-only 23D, and the complete 23D method are trained separately so that weights are never mixed across input semantics or completion environments.

\method{} only orders and selects valid rows already generated by SCIP, and SPBS submits only solutions checked in the complete model. Neither changes the objective, constraints, integrality, or bound logic. The learned components therefore change the search path and finite-time bound convergence, whereas feasibility and optimality retain the same meaning as in default SCIP.

\section{Computational experiments and results}

The experiments address three manufacturing questions: how full occupancy and dummy use affect mode preference across lot sizes and cleaning conditions; how much waiting and cycle coordination can improve while preserving product makespan; and whether limited computation yields a valid schedule in time. Feasible-schedule availability and product makespan measure usability and batch efficiency; waiting and cadence measure timing quality; gap and PDI explain search behavior. Residency uniformity, compliance with real-time JIT demand, and closed-loop execution under hardware deviations are not measured by the existing experiments.

\paragraph{Cleaning-policy revision and scope of evidence.}
The current model permits cleaning before the threshold, requires it at the threshold, and uses the expanded capacities in Eq.~\eqref{eq:candidate-bounds-en}. Section~\ref{sec:optional-evidence-en} reports newly solved and validated results under this rule. Subsequent historical benchmarks retain the numerical results for their original rules, fixed layouts, and budgets, and are explicitly identified in headings and captions. Historical optimality certificates do not transfer directly to the expanded feasible region. New conclusions rely on schedules passing both original-model and independent physical checks; a timeout without an incumbent or an execution failure is not an infeasibility proof.

Revalidation under the current rule uses the joint model with clean/dirty-hand constraints. Historical experiments separately cover the homogeneous-hand fixed-layout benchmark and joint scheduling with hand eligibility, the latter in Section~\ref{sec:joint-evidence-en}. Results under different rules, layouts, travel times, hand constraints, and resource budgets are reported separately and are not pooled for algorithm comparisons.

\subsection{Experimental design and metrics}

Historical experiments used a Windows~11 workstation with an Intel Core Ultra~7 265KF processor, 32~GB RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX~5060~Ti GPU with 8~GB device memory, with SCIP~10.0.1, PySCIPOpt~6.1.0, PyTorch~2.11.0, and Python~3.12.12. Revalidation under the current rule uses the same workstation and freezes the model, configuration, and policy checkpoints for each campaign; campaign records specify the applicable versions. Solve budgets and initialization costs are accounted for separately within each comparison family.

Historical benchmarks first evaluate mode and solver performance for prescribed structures under dual-source supply ($\sigma=1$); Section~\ref{sec:joint-evidence-en} records historical joint-model algorithm comparisons, mode sweeps, source routes, and parameter variations. Historical evidence includes 960 paired SPBS runs, 1500 equal-budget refinement runs, 300 factorial runs, 80 one-factor perturbation runs, and 729 distribution-shift runs across all methods on 40--64-product lots. These historical sample sizes are excluded from current-rule revalidation coverage. The experiments examine mode comparisons, waiting and cadence, feasible-schedule availability, bound convergence, and scale effects; the metrics below apply within each matched comparison family.

The compared methods use the same MILP, SCIP version, instances, and random seeds, and each comparison family uses common time and memory limits. Let $P(t)$ denote the primal bound of the minimization problem at time $t$, namely the objective value of the best incumbent, and let $D(t)$ denote the dual bound, namely the global lower bound certified by the search tree. Define $\Delta(t)=|P(t)-D(t)|$, $m(t)=\min\{|P(t)|,|D(t)|\}$, and $M(t)=\max\{|P(t)|,|D(t)|\}$. When the two bounds are finite, have the same sign, and differ, the terminal relative primal--dual gap is defined in SCIP as
\begin{equation}
G_{\rm end}=100\,\frac{\Delta(T)}{m(T)}.
\label{eq:terminal-gap-en}
\end{equation}
SCIP sets $G_{\rm end}=0$ when the bounds coincide and returns $+\infty$ when they have opposite signs or valid bounds are not both available. Equation~\eqref{eq:terminal-gap-en} normalizes by the smaller absolute bound and consequently does not cap $G_{\rm end}$ at 100\%. A value above 100\% indicates that the absolute bound difference exceeds the smaller absolute bound.

SCIP uses a different bounded normalization for the primal--dual integral. Define the instantaneous integral gap as
\begin{equation}
g_{\rm PDI}(t)=100\,\frac{\Delta(t)}{M(t)},\qquad
\pdi=\int_0^T g_{\rm PDI}(t)\,\mathrm dt.
\label{eq:pdi-gap-en}
\end{equation}

Here $t\in[0,T]$ is elapsed solve time and $T$ is the PDI horizon in seconds, distinct from the production-cycle capacity bound in Section 2. The functions $P(t),D(t)$ are the current primal upper and dual lower bounds; $\Delta(t)$ is their absolute difference and $m(t),M(t)$ the smaller and larger absolute bounds. The quantities $G_{\rm end}$ and $g_{\rm PDI}(t)$ denote terminal relative gap and instantaneous integral gap. The subscripts end and PDI are metric labels; PDI has units of percentage points times seconds.

Equation~\eqref{eq:pdi-gap-en} applies when the bounds are finite, have the same sign, and differ; SCIP sets $g_{\rm PDI}(t)=0$ for coincident bounds and $g_{\rm PDI}(t)=100$ otherwise. The instantaneous integral gap is therefore bounded to $[0,100]$; lower PDI means that the bound gap closes earlier. PDI is zero only if the gap is zero for almost the entire timed horizon, whereas $G_{\rm end}=0$ only states that optimality is proved at termination. Both quantities follow SCIP definitions and use its solution statistics \citep{achterberg2009scip,bestuzheva2023scip}.

Historical benchmarks use manufacturing instances as the inference unit: random repetitions are first aggregated within each instance, followed by nonparametric 95\% bootstrap intervals and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on paired instance differences, with Holm correction within prespecified analysis families; $p$ denotes a test $p$-value. Current-rule revalidation reports only paired observations and within-configuration summaries, without significance inference. Time-limited runs remain in their respective summaries, and random seeds are not treated as independent manufacturing instances.

For 16 wafers, the preferred mode changes with the interval. At $H=3$, pure $2\times2$ and mixed operation each contain two internal cleans, and pure $4\times1$ is preferred at 944~s. At $H=6$--10, all modes avoid cleaning; the pure $2\times2$ optimum of 898~s is 4.87\% and 4.37\% shorter than pure $4\times1$ and mixed operation, respectively.

The layout splits these into 2+2 PWs at $H=3$, 3+1 at $H=4$, and one complete chain at $H\ge5$. Although $H=3$ and 4 have equal internal-cleaning counts, their supply and return orders differ; the latter layout yields 1470~s. Mode selection therefore depends on lot size, recipe composition, and admissible complete-chain length. The experiments reconstruct preset complete-chain layouts for each $H$, so the comparison reflects the joint effect of the cleaning limit and chain layout.

\subsection{Historical benchmark: Search-stage diagnostics with strengthened bounds}

For the dual-source mixed configurations with 8 and 16 products and $H=3$, default SCIP and the complete selector are each run for 120~s with a common feasible initial schedule and identical strengthened bounds. These comprise analytical lower bounds from mandatory routes and resource occupancy and a makespan upper bound from the initial schedule. The four diagnostic runs are separate from the preceding 300-s comparisons and identify the stages consuming the search budget.

For eight products, both methods retain a feasible makespan of 2300~s and terminate with a lower bound of 492~s. Both enter the first root LP solve, executing 25630 and 24675 LP iterations, respectively, before any learned cut-selection call. For 16 products, both retain a feasible makespan of 4221~s and spend the 120-s budget in presolve, with zero processed nodes and zero LP solves. All four final schedules pass independent physical validation. These observations locate the computational effort in presolve and root relaxation, identifying the large discrete formulation and continuous-relaxation solution as the initial search costs for these joint instances.

\subsection{Discussion of mode selection and computational performance}

\paragraph{Conditional process-mode selection.} Full occupancy makes dummy supply and return part of production organization, requiring mode comparisons to include filling, complete chains, and cleaning occupancy. In the historical fixed-layout benchmark for 16 products, pure $4\times1$ is preferred at $H=3$ with 944~s, the mixture at $H=4$ with 939~s, and pure $2\times2$ at $H=6$ with 898~s. The decision concerns system makespan under specified conditions, rather than exposure duration alone. These findings apply to the original cleaning rule and threshold-dependent prescribed chain layouts. The same comparison principle applies to the current joint model, but insufficient bound separation prevents transferring historical switching points into universal rules for that model.

Current-rule experiments support three distinct conclusions: common initial solutions help the tested time-limited runs obtain feasible schedules; timing refinement after fixing the principal structure reduces waiting; and an alternative auxiliary-identity structure admits a feasible schedule strictly better than the original fixed-structure optimum. The 410-s structural example, waiting reductions from refinement, and feasible-schedule availability from initialization answer different questions and cannot be combined into a claim of superiority for the complete joint algorithm.

Expanding cleaning choices includes the choices permitted by the original rule at the model level, but time-limited heuristic search does not guarantee a better objective. Current matched runs do not show a makespan improvement from early cleaning, and mode-sweep lower bounds do not establish a preferred mode. Historical fixed-layout rankings established by optima or separated bounds apply only to their original rules and equipment settings.

\paragraph{Stable coordination and limits of engineering applicability.} Equal-budget refinement demonstrates reduced waiting within a selected structure; it does not establish reduced residency variance or strict JIT operation. Both hardware-deviation mechanisms motivate updates as parameters and states change, but a deterministic feasible solution obtained within a time limit does not establish execution feasibility under deviations. Deployment would additionally require measured duration and resource differences, state carryover at disruptions, rescheduling latency, and validation of execution constraints. These remain future validation tasks rather than completed results.

Learned cut selection under the current rule does not improve the common initial primary objective; some runs never invoke the custom selector, and callback counts are limited. The eight refinement configurations have no valid cadence-evaluation centers, so zero deviation cannot support cadence improvement. Further validation requires configurations with multiple production segments, complete mode coverage, and matched resource budgets. Unfinished sensitivity experiments remain pending until acceptance.

\section{Conclusions}

Starting from dummy occupancy required by full-occupancy processing, this study addresses conditional process-mode comparison and limited-budget scheduling in PECVD rotating-chamber cluster tools. The joint model connects product pairing, dummy loans, chamber assignment, complete chains, and cleaning boundaries within one physical timeline to evaluate two prescribed modes and their mixtures. Historical fixed-layout results for 16 products show preference switching between both pure modes and mixed operation as the cleaning threshold changes. Mode selection must therefore consider lot size, cleaning location, and resource coordination together; these switching results remain specific to the historical configurations.

Under the current rule, core validation, mode sweeping, and component ablation accept 112/126, 192/216, and 216/216 valid schedules, respectively. Across four matched core configurations, all 12 cold-start runs time out without a feasible solution, whereas all 12 serial-initialized and all 12 SPBS-initialized runs obtain feasible schedules. Equal-budget refinement over eight configurations with three seeds each preserves product makespan and reduces total route waiting by 22.29\%--50.25\%. The four-product dual-source auxiliary-identity comparison yields a feasible 410-s schedule below the proven 532-s optimum of the original fixed structure, showing that auxiliary-loan relationships materially affect attainable makespan.

The two stages separate product-batch efficiency from operating coordination: product-return makespan is optimized under residency and other physical constraints, then its bound is preserved while waiting and cycle-interval variation are reduced. This priority defines efficiency and timing quality explicitly, but does not directly optimize residency uniformity. Obtaining and validating a feasible schedule underpins the algorithm, while the contribution of learned search must be established under matched budgets. Action-duration deviations and resource-performance differences motivate further work on parameter updates and online scheduling.

These findings do not establish global optimality of the full joint problem, a necessary makespan reduction from early cleaning, or superiority of learned cut selection over baselines. The mode sweep has initialization memory failures and overlapping bounds in completed comparisons; refinement samples lack cadence-evaluation centers, and sensitivity experiments remain unfinished. Historical fixed-layout and original-rule results are retained separately and cannot substitute for evidence on these pending claims. Future work must complete mode coverage, include configurations with valid cadence structure, and retrain and compare policies under the current rule with matched settings.
