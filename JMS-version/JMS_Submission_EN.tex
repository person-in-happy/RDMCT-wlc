\author[ustb]{Lizhen Shao\corref{cor1}}
\ead{lshao@ustb.edu.cn}
\author[ustb]{Lincong Wei}
\ead{m202420836@xs.ustb.edu.cn}
\author[ustb]{Wei Wang}

\begin{abstract}
PECVD cluster tools with multi-position rotating chambers require coordinated scheduling of product wafers, dummy wafers, and periodic cleaning. Material origins, processing modes, and robot-hand eligibility jointly affect finite-batch operation. This study develops a two-stage mixed-integer linear programming model that jointly determines wafer pairing, dummy allocation, chamber assignment, resource order, and event times. The model represents single- and dual-source supply routes and distinguishes clean and dirty hands of the vacuum transfer robot. The first stage minimizes product-return makespan; the second refines waiting and cadence while retaining the makespan bound and principal discrete structure. Structured initialization and learning-enhanced cut selection support time-limited solving. All clean/dirty-hand experiments yield independently validated physical schedules. In a four-product structural comparison, reassigning dummy identities and reoptimizing timing creates 111~s of concurrent chamber processing and reduces makespan from the original fixed-structure optimum of 532~s to 403~s, a reduction of 24.25\%. Across six single- and dual-source configurations under the same 300-s total solve budget, timing refinement reduces total transport-route waiting by 8.40\%--29.21\% and shortens actual product makespan. Mode sweeps for lots of 8, 16, and 24 wafers further compare feasible schedules across cleaning intervals. These results quantify the effects of dummy-loan arrangements and timing optimization within a structure on finite-batch operation.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD) is used in semiconductor thin-film fabrication. Cluster tools (CTs) connect process modules, robots, and load locks into a continuous wafer-handling route. Processing and transport times, load-lock switching, cleaning, and residency constraints jointly determine feasible operation and equipment output \citep{pan2018survey}. Three-dimensional device structures and multilayer integration increase demands on deposition uniformity and processing capability \citep{appliedprecisioncvd}. Multi-space process modules and multi-finger robots provide a structural basis for parallel processing within a tool \citep{gu2024applied,gu2024multifinger}.

This study considers a PECVD CT with four-position rotating chambers. Rotation changes the positions accessible to the robot, allowing processing to alternate with paired loading and unloading. Products enter from atmospheric load ports, pass through alignment and load-lock transitions, and reach the chambers through the vacuum transfer robot. Dummies are stored in vacuum and reused to satisfy the full-occupancy and cleaning requirements of the prescribed recipe. We call the separate atmospheric product supply and vacuum dummy supply dual-source operation, and their interleaved use of shared robots and chambers mixed flow.

Full occupancy couples product supply with dummy reuse. Dummies fill positions when products cannot occupy the entire chamber. Cleaning occupies dummies and chambers and changes their subsequent availability. Product arrival therefore does not alone permit processing: dummy availability, robot service order, and cleaning boundaries must also be satisfied. The two material routes in Fig.~\ref{fig:system-en-new} require coordinated scheduling.

Two preset modes are considered: $4\times1$ parallel processing and $2\times2$ chained processing. The former processes four wafers synchronously once; the latter uses paired transfers and rotation to give each product two consecutive exposures. Each product recipe is prescribed in the instance and remains unchanged during scheduling; mode comparisons use separate instances with equal cumulative nominal processing time. Pair composition, chamber assignment, and cycle placement determine dummy requirements, chamber loads, and cleaning triggers, while shared robots constrain when these arrangements can be executed. This study therefore addresses joint pairing, chamber assignment, and event timing for given recipes and source configurations, and compares operating modes across lot sizes and cleaning intervals.

CT studies established analytical relationships between processing, handling, and throughput \citep{perkinson1994throughput} and used linear programming, heuristics, and timed Petri nets to obtain schedules under residency constraints \citep{rostami2002optimal,wu2010closedform,qiao2012sojourn}. Later models address revisiting processes, multiple wafer types, and selectable modules \citep{qiao2014revisiting,wang2020multiple,wang2025virtualcell}. Clean/dirty-hand separation and steady-state analysis of four-finger robots and four-space modules, together with joint optimization of two-type wafer releases and handling, supports more complex configurations \citep{gu2024multifinger,yang2025miptwowafers}. Finite batches additionally involve chain initialization, termination, and partial-batch filling, making dummy return times direct constraints on subsequent processing.

Periodic-cleaning scheduling uses task-sequence enumeration, partial loading and timing control, virtual-wafer occupancy, and chamber-state-dependent triggers to coordinate maintenance and production \citep{kim2013cleaning,yu2020delay,qiao2022binary,li2022conditioncleaning}. Shared-buffer synchronization and dynamic action screening address deadlock during failure shutdown and noncyclic operation \citep{zhu2025failuredeadlock,kim2025noncyclicdeadlock}; waiting constraints also affect equipment output and upstream control \citep{lee2018waiting}. Here, cleaning simultaneously changes chain segmentation, dummy occupancy, and vacuum-robot order. Scheduling product processing or cleaning independently would omit these dependencies.

These material and resource constraints define a mixed-integer scheduling problem. Learning can select CT actions directly or assist mathematical optimization. Reinforcement learning with look-ahead search supports noncyclic CT control \citep{kim2025adaptivecluster}; reinforcement-learning selection, cut ranking, and look-ahead imitation select candidate inequalities in MILP \citep{tang2020cut,huang2022cutranking,paulus2022looking}. HEM jointly determines cut counts and ordered sequences \citep{wang2023hem}. We retain this hierarchical decomposition and use equipment-variable structure for cut selection while representing process feasibility in the MILP.

This study jointly models product supply, dummy reuse, complete processing chains, and cleaning boundaries, and separately evaluates schedule construction and search strategies through paired experiments. Its contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item A joint pairing, dummy-allocation, chamber-assignment, and timing model coordinates cross-LP pairing of same-recipe products, product--dummy pairing, both supply routes, and clean/dirty VTR-hand eligibility. Cycle occupancy, first/last exposures, and side assignments directly represent full occupancy, complete chains, and cleaning boundaries; a controlled dummy-identity comparison tests the structural benefit of cross-chamber loan arrangements.
\item A makespan-first two-stage MILP refines waiting and cadence while retaining the first-stage makespan bound and principal discrete structure, with schedules evaluated across lot sizes and cleaning intervals.
\item SPBS initialization and role-aware hierarchical cut selection are embedded in the solver. Equal-budget comparisons separately evaluate initialization, timing refinement, and cut selection in terms of feasible-schedule availability and solution quality.
\end{enumerate}

Section~2 defines material routes, process constraints, and the two-stage model. Section~3 presents initialization and cut selection. Section~4 analyzes mode choice, schedule quality, and computational performance. Section~5 concludes.

\section{Problem description and two-stage scheduling model}
\setcounter{equation}{0}

\subsection{Equipment configuration and scheduling task}

Given product counts and recipes at LP1--LP4, dummy inventory, and the initial equipment state, scheduling jointly determines wafer pairing, dummy identities, chamber assignment, shared-resource order, and the start and finish times of loading, alignment, handling, rotation, processing, cleaning, and return actions. Products depart from LPs and return after processing. Dummies depart from the storage region specified by the source configuration, perform filling or cleaning tasks, and return. Single-source storage is beside LP4 in the atmospheric domain; dual-source storage is in the vacuum domain. Robot service, chamber occupancy, and dummy inventory couple the routes.

Inputs comprise action durations, resource capacities, residency limits, cleaning intervals, and dummy counts. Stage one minimizes the final product return time. Stage two improves waiting and cadence under the resulting makespan bound. Each feasible schedule is represented by an action sequence specifying wafers, resources, and start and completion times for performance evaluation and checks of precedence and resource conflicts.

The cleaning interval satisfies $H\ge2$, and the shared inventory contains at least four dummies. Action durations are deterministic within a solve. No buffers are assumed beyond LPs, aligners, load locks, rotating chambers, and vacuum storage, so each wait corresponds to a material location and resource-occupancy interval.

\subsection{PW-pair modeling for the two process modes}

To represent wafer-level residency and paired handling together, shared transfer and chamber occupancy are modeled at the pair level, while alignment and route timing retain wafer-level events.

A wafer pair has two member positions and may contain two products, a product and a dummy, or two dummies. Product load ports $\ell_w$ and recipes $m_w$ are inputs; products with the same recipe may be paired across LPs. Members of candidate pairs $\mathcal P$ are selected through binary variables $a_{wpr}$ for product membership at position $r\in\{0,1\}$, $d_{pr}$ for dummy occupancy, and $v_p$ for activation:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{p,r}a_{wpr}&=1 &&(w\in\mathcal W),\\
\sum_w a_{wpr}+d_{pr}&=v_p &&(p\in\mathcal P,\ r=0,1).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:joint-membership-en}
\end{equation}
Let $\mathcal M=\{\zeta,\psi,\mathrm{cl}\}$, where $\zeta$ denotes $4\times1$, $\psi$ denotes $2\times2$, and $\mathrm{cl}$ denotes cleaning. The set $\mathcal C(m)$ contains chambers compatible with mode $m$. Binary variables $\mu_{pm}$ and $z_{pc}$ select the pair recipe and chamber:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\sum_m\mu_{pm}&=v_p, &\sum_c z_{pc}&=v_p,\\
a_{wpr}&\le\mu_{p,m_w},\\
z_{pc}+\mu_{pm}&\le1 &&(c\notin\mathcal C(m)).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:joint-chamber-en}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:joint-membership-en} and \eqref{eq:joint-chamber-en} assign each product once and fill both positions of every active pair. For $c\notin\mathcal C(m)$, the final inequality prohibits selecting mode $m$ and chamber $c$ simultaneously. Product recipe $m_w$ and source configuration $\sigma$ are inputs; pairing, chamber assignment, and processing order are decisions. Binary quantities $B_p$ and $B_p^{\psi}$ identify product-containing pairs and pairs containing $2\times2$ products:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
B_p&\le\sum_{w,r}a_{wpr}\le2B_p,\qquad B_p+\mu_{p,\mathrm{cl}}\le1,\\
B_p^{\psi}&\le B_p,\quad B_p^{\psi}\le\mu_{p\psi},\quad
B_p^{\psi}\ge B_p+\mu_{p\psi}-1.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:pair-type-en}
\end{equation}
Dummy-only pairs provide filling or cleaning; product-containing pairs cannot perform cleaning. Membership, cycle occupancy, and event links directly represent the joint structure without enumerating complete schedules. For $4\times1$ and $2\times2$ product counts $N_{\zeta},N_{\psi}$, respectively, candidate capacities are
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
T=N_{\zeta}+2N_{\psi},\qquad J_{\max}=\lfloor T/(H-1)\rfloor,\\
P_{\max}=N+T+2J_{\max},\qquad Q_{\max}=T+J_{\max}.
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:candidate-bounds-en}
\end{equation}
$T$ bounds productive cycles because every productive cycle contains a product, and each $4\times1$ or $2\times2$ product appears once or twice, respectively. Each cleaning follows at least $H-1$ productive cycles, giving $J_{\max}$. There are at most $N$ product-containing pairs, one dummy-only support pair per productive cycle, and two dummy-only pairs per cleaning, giving $P_{\max}$. Each chamber receives $Q_{\max}$ candidate cycles, with a shared bound on total productive and cleaning cycles. These capacities bound candidate indices; optimization determines how many are activated. Prefix activation of pair labels and canonical member ordering remove interchangeable labels without prescribing physical processing order.

A product follows LP--ATR--AL--ATR--upper load lock--VTR--rotary chamber--VTR--lower load lock--ATR--LP: the ATR first places the wafer on the aligner, then retrieves it for transfer into the vacuum domain. Under dual-source supply, a dummy travels from vacuum storage through the VTR to the chamber and returns through the VTR after use; under single-source supply, it moves from storage beside LP4 through ATR directly to the upper load lock. When collected with a product, the dummy accompanies the ATR to AL and remains on its gripper until the aligned product is retrieved; both then enter the upper load lock together. The dummy itself requires no alignment. A regular all-product $4\times1$ batch contains two product PW pairs, hence four product wafers, and processes all four positions synchronously once before last-in--first-out unloading; dummy or mixed PW pairs fill any remaining positions. In $2\times2$, every product-containing PW pair occupies two adjacent process cycles so that each of its product members is processed twice. For three product PW pairs, a dummy PW pair D is loaded first, the chamber rotates, and P1 is loaded before D+P1 undergoes chamber processing. Chamber processing then alternates with ``chamber rotation + chain slide'', changing the occupancy successively to P1+P2, P2+P3, and P3+D; P1, P2, and P3 are each processed twice before all wafers are unloaded. Figures~\ref{fig:chain-en} and \ref{fig:parallel-en} show the event sequences.

\subsection{Material-source configuration and time relations}

Let $\mathcal S=\{A,V\}$ denote atmospheric and vacuum supply regions, respectively. The input parameter $\sigma\in\{0,1\}$ specifies single-source and dual-source configurations, respectively. LP1--LP4 are individual atmospheric cassette locations; each product wafer has a prescribed load port. Supply regions and cassette indices therefore describe different levels. Products satisfy $\chi_{iA}=1$ and $\chi_{iV}=0$, whereas dummies satisfy $\chi_{iA}=1-\sigma$ and $\chi_{iV}=\sigma$. The source-incidence parameters satisfy
\begin{equation}
\sum_{s\in\mathcal S}\chi_{is}=1,
\qquad i\in\mathcal I.
\label{eq:source-membership-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:source-membership-en} specifies the supply region of each material unit. In the single-source configuration, products occupy LP1--LP4 and dummies occupy atmospheric storage beside LP4. Both use ATR and the load-lock interface, but only products require alignment. In the dual-source configuration, products originate from LP1--LP4 while dummies originate from vacuum storage. Single-source thus denotes atmospheric supply of both wafer types, rather than a single cassette.

Let $\mathcal A^{\rm int}$ denote common in-chamber precedences and $\mathcal A_{is}(\gamma)$ the supply/return arcs of material unit $i$ from source $s$ under transfer choices $\gamma$. Material type and paired handling jointly determine the activated routes:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal A=\mathcal A^{\rm int}\cup
\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal I}\ \bigcup_{\substack{s\in\mathcal S:\\ \chi_{is}=1}}\mathcal A_{is}(\gamma).
\label{eq:route-union-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:route-union-en} selects routes by material unit. Products pass through LP, ATR, AL, ATR, the upper load lock, VTR, and a chamber. A separately supplied single-source dummy follows atmospheric storage, ATR, the upper load lock, VTR, and a chamber. When collected with a product, the dummy remains on an ATR gripper during the AL visit; this creates gripper occupancy but no dummy placement, retrieval, or alignment event at AL. Joint placement in the load lock follows product retrieval from AL. Both atmospheric wafer types return through VTR, the lower load lock, and ATR to their original storage locations. Dual-source dummies travel only between vacuum storage and a chamber through VTR.



For each candidate operation $o$, activation and duration satisfy
\begin{equation}
0\le s_o\le Mb_o,\qquad
0\le e_o\le Mb_o,\qquad
e_o=s_o+d_ob_o.
\label{eq:operation-time-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:operation-time-en} sets inactive operation times to zero and gives an active operation duration $d_o$. It applies to robot moves, alignment, pump/vent, rotation, product processing, dummy transport, and cleaning.

For every required process arc $(o,o')\in\mathcal A$,
\begin{equation}
b_{o'}\le b_o,\qquad
s_{o'}\ge e_o+\delta_{oo'}-M(2-b_o-b_{o'}).
\label{eq:precedence-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:precedence-en} activates a successor only with its predecessor and, when both exist, starts it after the predecessor and minimum lag. These arcs connect product routes, dummy routes, and load--process--unload chains.

\subsection{Joint cycle occupancy and rotating-chamber transfers}

Let $\mathcal Q_c=\{1,\ldots,Q_{\max}\}$ index candidate cycles of chamber $c$, including both production modes and cleaning. Binary variables $U_{cq}$, $K_{cqm}$, and $X_{pcq}$ select cycle activation, cycle type, and participating PW pairs. Every active cycle contains two pairs, with compatible recipes and chambers:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\sum_mK_{cqm}&=U_{cq}, &\sum_pX_{pcq}&=2U_{cq},\\
X_{pcq}&\le z_{pc}, &X_{pcq}+\mu_{pm}&\le1+K_{cqm},\\
\sum_pX^P_{pcq}&\ge K_{cq\zeta}+K_{cq\psi}, &U_{c,q+1}&\le U_{cq},\\
\sum_{c,q}U_{cq}&\le Q_{\max}, &\sum_{c,q}K_{cq,\mathrm{cl}}&\le J_{\max}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cycle-capacity-en}
\end{equation}
The indicator $X^P_{pcq}$ identifies a participating product-containing pair through
\begin{equation}
X^P_{pcq}\le X_{pcq},\quad X^P_{pcq}\le B_p,\quad
X^P_{pcq}\ge X_{pcq}+B_p-1.
\label{eq:cycle-products-en}
\end{equation}
Every productive cycle therefore contains a product pair, whereas cleaning contains only dummy pairs. Prefix activation eliminates empty indices without prescribing the order of different recipes.

Binary variables $A_{pcq},Z_{pcq}$ identify the first and last cycles of pair $p$ in chamber $c$. With boundary values \mbox{$X_{pc0}=X_{pc,Q_{\max}+1}=0$},
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
A_{pcq}&\ge X_{pcq}-X_{pc,q-1}, &A_{pcq}&\le X_{pcq},\\
A_{pcq}&\le1-X_{pc,q-1},\\
Z_{pcq}&\ge X_{pcq}-X_{pc,q+1}, &Z_{pcq}&\le X_{pcq},\\
Z_{pcq}&\le1-X_{pc,q+1},\\
\sum_{c,q}X_{pcq}&=v_p+B_p^{\psi}, &\sum_{c,q}A_{pcq}&=\sum_{c,q}Z_{pcq}=v_p,\\
\sum_pA^{\psi}_{pcq}&\le1, &\sum_pZ^{\psi}_{pcq}&\le1.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cycle-exposures-en}
\end{equation}
Here $A^{\psi}_{pcq}=A_{pcq}\land B_p^{\psi}$ and $Z^{\psi}_{pcq}=Z_{pcq}\land B_p^{\psi}$ use the three-inequality linearization in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cycle-products-en}. Each active pair enters and exits once, so occupancy is contiguous. Its total occupancy $v_p+B_p^{\psi}$ gives one cycle for a $4\times1$ product pair and two adjacent exposures in the same chamber for a $2\times2$ product pair. At most one $2\times2$ product pair starts or finishes exposure in each cycle, producing the alternating exchange chain. Each dummy-only support or cleaning pair occupies one cycle.

Cycle processing starts and ends at $S_{cq},E_{cq}$; $d_m$ is the processing duration of mode $m$ and $\overline T$ is a valid time horizon:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
E_{cq}-S_{cq}&=\sum_m d_mK_{cqm},\\
0\le S_{cq},E_{cq}&\le\overline T U_{cq},\\
S_{cq}&\ge E_{c,q-1}-M(1-U_{cq})\quad(q>1).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cycle-time-en}
\end{equation}
Processing durations are fixed, with waiting confined to intervals between operations. Defaults are 180~s for $4\times1$, 90~s per $2\times2$ exposure, and 500~s for cleaning treatment; loading, unloading, and rotation retain their separate event durations.

Let binary $f_{pcq}$ identify the pair on the robot-accessible front side during processing, $\kappa_{cq}$ indicate retention of one pair from the preceding cycle, and $\upsilon_{cq}$ indicate complete departure after the current cycle:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\kappa_{cq}&=\sum_p(X_{pcq}-A_{pcq}),\\
\upsilon_{cq}&=U_{cq}-\sum_p(X_{pcq}-Z_{pcq}),\\
f_{pcq}&\le A_{pcq},\qquad\sum_pf_{pcq}=U_{cq},\\
f_{pc,q-1}&\ge X_{pcq}-A_{pcq}\quad(q>1),\\
f_{pcq}&\ge B_p+K_{cq\psi}+X_{pcq}-2-\kappa_{cq}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cycle-side-en}
\end{equation}
The newly loaded pair occupies the front; a retained pair rotates to the back and must have occupied the front in the preceding cycle. At a fresh $2\times2$ chain head, the product pair loads last at the front. Pair loading/unloading times reuse their physical events. Pre- and post-cycle half-rotations, denoted $\mathrm{rot-}$ and $\mathrm{rot+}$, are activated by $U_{cq}$ and $\upsilon_{cq}$, respectively, and last $r_c$. Loading is linked by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
X_{pcq}=1&\Rightarrow S_{cq}\ge e^{\rm load}_p,\quad
s^{\rm unload}_p\ge E_{cq},\\
A_{pcq}=1,\ f_{pcq}=0&\Rightarrow e^{\rm load}_p\le s^{\rm rot-}_{cq},\\
A_{pcq}=1,\ f_{pcq}=1&\Rightarrow s^{\rm load}_p\ge e^{\rm rot-}_{cq},\quad
S_{cq}=e^{\rm load}_p,\\
\kappa_{cq}=1&\Rightarrow s^{\rm rot-}_{cq}\ge E_{c,q-1}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cycle-load-en}
\end{equation}
When all pairs depart, post-cycle rotation starts after processing. Unloading after a pair's final cycle satisfies
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
Z_{pcq}=1,\ f_{pcq}=1&\Rightarrow e^{\rm unload}_p\le s^{\rm rot+}_{cq},\\
Z_{pcq}=1,\ f_{pcq}=0,\ \upsilon_{cq}=1
&\Rightarrow s^{\rm unload}_p\ge e^{\rm rot+}_{cq},\\
Z_{pcq}=1,\ f_{pcq}=0,\ \upsilon_{cq}=0
&\Rightarrow s^{\rm unload}_p\ge e^{\rm rot-}_{c,q+1}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cycle-unload-en}
\end{equation}
If the next cycle retains one pair, incoming loading waits for removal of the replaced back-side pair. If no pair is retained, loading waits for both pairs from the preceding cycle to be unloaded. Indicator constraints or valid big-$M$ inequalities linearize these conditions; VTR exclusion additionally constrains the exchange. Waiting is allowed and remains separate from fixed rotation and handling durations.

In a selected schedule, $x^{\zeta}_{pcbl},x^{\psi}_{pck}$ record $4\times1$ batch sides and $2\times2$ product-pair chain positions derived from $X,A,Z,f$ and cycle types. Batches, chains, and cleaning segments describe the schedule and the fixed-layout benchmark without introducing independent pairing decisions. Fixed-layout experiments additionally fix positions and service orders and restrict dummy filling to the last batch: a $4\times1$ batch preceding another batch of that type contains two product pairs. The joint model permits dummy filling in any admissible productive cycle.


\subsection{Cleaning and reusable dummy wafers}

Integer $n_{cq}$ counts productive cycles since the last cleaning at the end of cycle $q$. With terminal candidate values $U_{c,Q_{\max}+1}=K_{c,Q_{\max}+1,\psi}=0$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
0\le n_{cq}&\le H U_{cq},\qquad n_{c0}=0,\\
K_{cq,\mathrm{cl}}=1&\Rightarrow n_{cq}=0,\\
U_{cq}=1,\ K_{cq,\mathrm{cl}}=0
&\Rightarrow n_{cq}=n_{c,q-1}+1,\quad n_{c,q-1}\le H-1,\\
K_{cq,\mathrm{cl}}=1&\Rightarrow n_{c,q-1}\ge H-1,\quad
n_{c,q-1}+K_{c,q+1,\psi}\ge H,\\
n_{cq}&\le H-1+H U_{c,q+1},\\
K_{c1,\mathrm{cl}}&=0,\qquad
K_{cq,\mathrm{cl}}+K_{c,q-1,\mathrm{cl}}\le1\quad(q>1).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:clean-counter-en}
\end{equation}
Production increments the count and cleaning resets it. Production cannot continue at count $H$; early cleaning at $H-1$ is allowed only when the next cycle is $2\times2$. The last active cycle has count at most $H-1$, requiring terminal cleaning when the final production segment reaches $H$. Cleaning cannot interrupt a pair's two exposures because occupancy and cycle type jointly satisfy Eqs.~\eqref{eq:cycle-capacity-en} and \eqref{eq:cycle-exposures-en}.

Cleaning segments $e$ are induced by cleaning boundaries in the cycle-type sequence. Indicators $z_{ce}$, $a^{\zeta}_{cbe},a^{\psi}_{cqe}$, and $q^{\rm cl}_{ce}$ identify active production segments, batch/exposure membership, and subsequent cleaning; $N_{ce}$ counts productive cycles. Internal segments end with cleaning, as does a final segment reaching $H$. These quantities describe event windows and vary with cycle decisions.



Let $\mathcal V_c$ be the union of $4\times1$ batches and $2\times2$ cycles and let $a_{vce}$ take the corresponding $a^{\zeta}$ or $a^{\psi}$. Cleaning order and the physical cleaning chain satisfy
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s_v&\ge e^{\rm cl}_{c,e-1}-M(1-a_{vce}) &&(e>1),\\
e_v&\le s^{\rm cl}_{ce}+M(1-a_{vce})+M(1-q^{\rm cl}_{ce}),\\
s^{\rm cl,BL}_{ce}&\ge e^{\rm cl,FL}_{ce}+r_c-M(1-q^{\rm cl}_{ce}),\\
s^{\rm cl,P}_{ce}&\ge e^{\rm cl,BL}_{ce}-M(1-q^{\rm cl}_{ce}),\\
s^{\rm cl,BU}_{ce}&\ge e^{\rm cl,P}_{ce}-M(1-q^{\rm cl}_{ce}),\\
s^{\rm cl,FU}_{ce}&\ge e^{\rm cl,BU}_{ce}+r_c-M(1-q^{\rm cl}_{ce}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:clean-time-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:clean-time-en} starts a segment after the preceding cleaning and finishes it before the next cleaning. A cleaning task loads both chamber sides with dummies, rotates, processes, and unloads in reverse, thereby occupying the VTR, chamber, and four physical dummies rather than adding virtual downtime.

Each dummy member position defines a loan demand $j=(p,r)$, activated directly by $d_{pr}$. This covers mixed product--dummy pairs, dummy-only filling pairs and cleaning pairs. The selected configuration specifies supply and return events; a physical-dummy loan extends from storage pickup through completed return.

Chambers access a shared physical inventory: $\mathcal T_c$ denotes the identities available to chamber $c$, and loans of the same identity are mutually exclusive across chambers. Each active demand selects one physical dummy from inventory:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal T_c}g_{j\tau}=d_{pr},
\qquad j=(p,r)\in\mathcal J_c^D.
\label{eq:dummy-assign-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:dummy-assign-en} assigns every active demand exactly once. The identity selection $g_{j\tau}$ also activates its full-loan resource-occupancy interval. Mutual exclusion orders repeated loans of the same physical dummy, so it can be reused only after unloading and return to storage. Identity selection determines which cross-chamber loans require ordering. Even with identical product pairing, chamber assignment, and cycle structure, different dummy identities can change the intervals of concurrent chamber processing. Section~\ref{sec:joint-evidence-en} quantifies this effect while controlling product structure.

\subsection{Robots, load locks, chambers, and time resources}

Shared-resource orders comprise prescribed and selectable precedences. Operations on process-precedence arcs and prescribed service chains follow their specified order; other conflicting operations are coordinated by ordering variables. The fixed-layout pure $4\times1$ experiments prescribe aligner and load-lock service orders. When the candidate batch count does not exceed the cleaning threshold, VTR loading and unloading also follow a prescribed batch-service chain. These prescribed orders apply to the fixed-layout experiments; in the joint model, ordering variables select the remaining resource precedences.

ATR has two hands, VTR has four hands, and each rotating chamber has four wafer positions, giving physical capacities of two, four, and four wafers, respectively. Each robot executes one coordinated handling action at a time, and each chamber executes one loading, unloading, rotation, or processing stage at a time. ATR, VTR, and each chamber actuator are therefore unary action resources; wafer-level hand assignments and pair occupancy represent their physical carrying capacities. AL is also a unary action resource. The upper load lock has slots U1 and U2, and the lower load lock has slots L1 and L2; each dummy identity is a reusable resource. Resource assignment is defined for individual occupancy intervals: a wafer-position occupancy, a dummy loan, or an exclusive actuator use selects one eligible unit. Denoting this resource-occupancy task by $o$,
\begin{equation}
\sum_{\ell\in\Gamma_o}h_{o\ell}=b_o.
\label{eq:slot-select-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:slot-select-en} assigns each active resource-occupancy task exactly one eligible unit. The binary indicator $b_o$ activates the task, $h_{o\ell}$ selects unit $\ell$, and $\Gamma_o$ contains eligible units; all assignments are zero for an inactive task. Wafers transferred as a pair retain individual wafer-position occupancies, with synchronized actions governed by Eq.~\eqref{eq:double-transfer-en}. PW-pair occupancy enforces the four-position chamber requirement; an actuator's exclusion unit does not represent wafer capacity. Equation~\eqref{eq:resource-order-en} prevents overlapping occupancy of a shared unit. For a singleton $\Gamma_o$, the identity $h_{o\ell}=b_o$ can be substituted directly without a separate assignment variable. Dummy-identity occupancy reuses $g_{j\tau}$ rather than introducing duplicate identity decisions.

Upper-LL occupancy extends from ATR placement to completion of VTR pickup plus reset; lower-LL occupancy extends from VTR placement to completion of ATR pickup plus reset. Reset restricts the next use of that slot and may overlap onward robot motion after pickup.

The VTR has two dirty hands, $D_1,D_2$, and two clean hands, $C_1,C_2$. For an unprocessed product transferred from the upper load lock to a chamber, $\Gamma_o=\{D_1,D_2\}$; for a fully processed product transferred from a chamber to the lower load lock, $\Gamma_o=\{C_1,C_2\}$. Dummy wafers may use $\Gamma_o=\{D_1,D_2,C_1,C_2\}$ on either transfer leg. Each occupancy interval extends from pickup start through placement completion and retains the same physical hand selected by Eq.~\eqref{eq:slot-select-en}. Equation~\eqref{eq:resource-order-en} excludes overlapping use of that hand. The separation between hand-occupancy intervals is zero; robot travel remains governed by the actuator-level travel constraints. Dirty and clean hands therefore each carry at most two wafers, with dummies competing for available hands of either type. A $2\times2$ product remains inside the chamber between its consecutive exposures and is retrieved by a clean hand only after the second exposure. During a paired exchange, dirty hands may hold the incoming product pair while clean hands retrieve the completed pair; every dummy must likewise occupy a specific available hand.

If operations $o$ and $o'$ may compete for slot $\ell$ of resource $r$, then
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s_{o'}&\ge e_o+\rho^r_{oo'}-M(1-y^r_{oo'})
-M(1-h_{o\ell})-M(1-h_{o'\ell}),\\
s_o&\ge e_{o'}+\rho^r_{o'o}-My^r_{oo'}
-M(1-h_{o\ell})-M(1-h_{o'\ell}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:resource-order-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:resource-order-en} makes $y^r_{oo'}$ choose one order whenever two actions use the same slot and includes robot repositioning or equipment reset through $\rho^r_{oo'}$. It applies to ATR, AL, both load locks, VTR, rotary chambers, and individual dummies, preventing concurrent robot actions and reuse before release.

Travel times are inputs for each location pair. When $z_{pc}$ determines an operation's chamber, the corresponding travel lag is activated for each candidate chamber combination. For active conflicting chamber operations $o,o'$ serving pairs $p,p'$, for example,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
z_{pc}=z_{p'c'}=1,\ y^{\rm VTR}_{oo'}=1
\ \Rightarrow\ s_{o'}\ge e_o+\rho^{\rm VTR}_{cc'}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:location-travel-en}
\end{equation}
Here $\rho^{\rm VTR}_{cc'}$ is the prescribed inter-chamber travel time; the reverse order is analogous. Fixed-location operations use Eq.~\eqref{eq:resource-order-en} directly.

Process precedences and selected resource orders form a directed event graph. A cycle of conflicting operations with positive durations or transitions would require a start time to be strictly later than itself and is excluded by timing feasibility. Together with explicit slot occupancy, full chamber occupancy, and complete dummy return, these constraints exclude circular waiting among the modeled operations.

An individual product or dummy stage must coincide with its selected batch, chain position, or cleaning window. For selection $x$ and reference stage $[s^{\rm ref},e^{\rm ref}]$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
-M(1-x)&\le s_o-s^{\rm ref}\le M(1-x),\\
-M(1-x)&\le e_o-e^{\rm ref}\le M(1-x).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:stage-link-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:stage-link-en} synchronizes the individual action with its physical stage when $x=1$ and releases the candidate relation when $x=0$, connecting pair positions, product routes, dummy demands, and chamber windows.



If pair $p$ uses one paired transfer on a compatible atomic handling leg $a$ at a common location, the two wafer stages are synchronized:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
-M(1-\gamma_{pa})&\le s_{w_1a}-s_{w_2a}\le M(1-\gamma_{pa}),\\
-M(1-\gamma_{pa})&\le e_{w_1a}-e_{w_2a}\le M(1-\gamma_{pa}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:double-transfer-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:double-transfer-en} means that $\gamma_{pa}=1$ moves a complete pair in one non-preemptive robot action. If $\gamma_{pa}=0$, the wafers still occupy the same robot separately and are ordered by Eq.~\eqref{eq:resource-order-en}. Actuator exclusion counts coordinated actions: the two wafer events of a paired transfer represent one action, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:resource-order-en} is not activated between them. The shared action remains mutually exclusive with other actions. Individual holding intervals are retained and physical-hand exclusion always applies; synchronizing actions does not merge wafer capacity.

For a single-source product--dummy pair, let $w_1$ denote the product and $w_2$ the dummy. The choice $\gamma_{p,\mathrm{in}}$ activates accompanied entry after their collection. This route-linking choice does not synchronize pickups at different storage locations. Source pickup, AL placement, AL retrieval, and upper-load-lock placement are labeled $\mathrm{pick,ALput,ALpick,LLput}$, respectively. Let $\gamma^{\rm early}_p,\gamma^{\rm late}_p$ select direct dummy entry before the AL visit or pickup after product placement at AL. Together with accompanied entry, exactly one is selected:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\gamma^{\rm early}_p+\gamma^{\rm late}_p+\gamma_{p,\mathrm{in}}=1,\\
\gamma^{\rm early}_p=1\Rightarrow e_{w_2,\mathrm{LLput}}\le s_{w_1,\mathrm{ALput}},\\
\gamma^{\rm late}_p=1\Rightarrow s_{w_2,\mathrm{pick}}\ge e_{w_1,\mathrm{ALput}},\\
\gamma_{p,\mathrm{in}}=1\ \Longrightarrow
\begin{cases}
e_{w_2,\mathrm{pick}}\le s_{w_1,\mathrm{ALput}},\\
s_{w_2,\mathrm{LLput}}=s_{w_1,\mathrm{LLput}},\\
e_{w_2,\mathrm{LLput}}=e_{w_1,\mathrm{LLput}},\\
s_{w_1,\mathrm{LLput}}\ge e_{w_1,\mathrm{ALpick}}.
\end{cases}\end{gathered}
\label{eq:single-source-accompany-en}
\end{equation}
The dummy is already carried when the product is placed at AL and enters the upper load lock simultaneously with the aligned product. It occupies one ATR gripper continuously from source pickup through load-lock placement. The product releases its gripper while on AL and occupies it again upon retrieval. Independent dummy entry instead directly links source pickup to load-lock placement. Neither alternative activates dummy alignment at AL.

For the entry and exit events of wafer $w$ in restricted equipment region $r$,
\begin{equation}
0\le s_{\operatorname{out}(w,r)}-e_{\operatorname{in}(w,r)}
\le\overline R_{wr}.
\label{eq:residence-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:residence-en} limits the physical stay in an upper load lock, chamber, lower load lock, or restricted transport region. Material occupancy of a load-lock slot ends when opposite-side pickup is complete. Pressure reset may then overlap subsequent robot travel; the next incompatible load-lock operation must wait until reset is complete.

\subsection{Makespan-first two-stage optimization}

Let $C_w$ be the time at which product wafer $w$ completes the lower-load-lock return and reaches its load port. Stage one solves
\begin{equation}
C_w=e_{w,\mathrm{return}},\qquad
C_{\max}\ge C_w\ (w\in\mathcal W),\qquad
\min_{\xi\in\mathcal F}C_{\max}.
\label{eq:primary-objective-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:primary-objective-en} measures makespan at the last product wafer's return to LP after its complete end-to-end route. Internal cleaning affects $C_{\max}$ through resource occupancy and precedence that delay subsequent product returns. Terminal cleaning remains in the action sequence, but its completion time does not directly lower-bound $C_{\max}$. It can still affect other chambers' product returns through competition for the shared VTR; this indirect effect is determined by the complete schedule. WPH uses product-return makespan as its denominator. The joint feasible region $\mathcal F$ combines membership, chamber assignment, cycle occupancy, and complete physical timing constraints. Fixed-layout experiments additionally impose their layout and service-order restrictions.

Given stage-one incumbent $\widehat\xi$ and makespan $\widehat C$, stage two fixes the discrete structure indexed by $\mathcal D_{\rm fix}$ and minimizes linear schedule quality within the makespan tolerance:
Here $\mathcal Q^w$ indexes the evaluated waits. Each $\omega_\alpha$ is the nonnegative remainder of its event interval after required processing or movement time is subtracted. $L_{\rm sum}$ sums these waits, including separate waits of different materials that occur concurrently, and $L_{\max}$ bounds the largest item.

For chamber $c$, cleaning segment $e$ is a production segment between cleanings, including production before the first and after the last cleaning. $h_{ceq}$ denotes the processing start of cycle $q$ within that segment and aliases the corresponding $s_o$, introducing no independent timing decision. $\mathcal I_h$ in the joint model contains three consecutive exposures in the same chamber and $2\times2$ chain, with a pair retained at both boundaries, $\kappa_{c,q+1}=\kappa_{cq}=1$; differences do not cross cleaning or chain boundaries. $\Delta_{ceq}$ subtracts the preceding start interval from the following one, and $v_{ceq}\ge0$ bounds its absolute value. Both have units of seconds and measure cadence variation rather than processing duration. The continuous time $h_{ceq}$ differs from binary assignment $h_{o\ell}$; likewise, $v_{ceq}$ differs from pair activation $v_p$ and cycle activation $U_{cq}$.

\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\xi_d=\widehat\xi_d\quad(d\in\mathcal D_{\rm fix}),\qquad
C_{\max}\le\widehat C+\varepsilon_C,\\
L_{\rm sum}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal Q^w}\omega_\alpha,\qquad
L_{\max}\ge\omega_\alpha,\\
\Delta_{ceq}=(h_{ce,q+1}-h_{ceq})-(h_{ceq}-h_{ce,q-1}),\\
v_{ceq}\ge\Delta_{ceq},\qquad v_{ceq}\ge-\Delta_{ceq},\\
\eta_\alpha\ge\omega_\alpha-\bar\omega_\alpha,\qquad
\eta_\alpha\ge0,\\
\min\Phi=\lambda_1L_{\rm sum}+\lambda_2L_{\max}
+\lambda_3\sum_{(c,e,q)\in\mathcal I_h}v_{ceq}
+\lambda_4\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal Q^w}\eta_\alpha.
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:secondary-objective-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:secondary-objective-en} retains the stage-one feasible makespan bound within the specified tolerance and fixes pairing, chamber and cycle assignments, dummy identities, hand assignments, accompanied-entry choices, and resource orders. Retained same-location atomic synchronization choices and continuous times remain adjustable to reduce total wait, maximum wait, and variation between consecutive processing intervals within each segment. Optional soft-limit excess can also be penalized. The experiments use $(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3)=(1,10,25)$ and $\varepsilon_C=10^{-6}$. With the soft waiting limit disabled, no excess variables are generated. For a time-limited first stage, the best incumbent value $\widehat C$ supplies the second-stage bound.

With strictly positive corresponding weights at a stage-two optimum, these upper bounds are tight: $L_{\max}=\max_\alpha\omega_\alpha$, $v_{ceq}=|\Delta_{ceq}|$, and $\eta_\alpha=\max(0,\omega_\alpha-\bar\omega_\alpha)$. Auxiliary bounds in a time-limited feasible solution may retain slack, so schedule metrics are recomputed from actual event times. For example, process starts at 100, 200, and 320~s within one segment give intervals of 100 and 120~s, hence $\Delta_{ceq}=20$~s and a minimum admissible $v_{ceq}$ of 20~s. Starts at 100, 210, and 320~s give zero variation. The cadence term penalizes interval changes, not interval length itself. Soft limits permit penalized excess, unlike hard residency constraints.

\section{Structure-aware MILP solution framework}

\label{sec:method-new}

Section 2 links membership and cycle occupancy to complete material routes in a joint model. The solution framework combines structured initialization, MILP search, and timing refinement for both the fixed-configuration special case and the joint model. The fixed-layout benchmark supports training and component comparisons; the joint model evaluates search with the frozen learned policy. Primal feasibility requires coordination of full occupancy, dual-source logistics, and shared-resource order, whereas dual-bound improvement depends on valid inequalities acting on discrete choices, continuous times, and their coupling. We exploit these structures through a structure-preserving binary skeleton (SPBS) for initial feasible scheduling and \method{} for cut selection. SPBS supplies a feasible starting point, and \method{} determines cut counts and insertion order. SCIP generates valid cuts, checks feasibility, maintains bounds, and certifies optimality \citep{achterberg2009scip,bestuzheva2023scip}.

The solution process comprises initial-schedule construction and validation, candidate-cut generation, count prediction, ordered-subset search, and role-submodular completion, followed by continued branch-and-bound search. All selected cuts are valid inequalities for the original model and retain their coefficients. Cut selection therefore controls the strengthening of the linear relaxation while preserving the original integer feasible set.

\subsection{SPBS initial solution}

For the evaluated fixed-configuration special case, SPBS retains its pairing, chamber and cleaning layout, constructs a skeleton for selectable resource orders and slots, and then solves for event times in a restricted MILP. Product wafers are paired in input order. The $4\times1$ pairs are mapped sequentially to chambers, batches, and front/back positions, whereas the $2\times2$ pairs are assigned round-robin to chamber-chain positions. This determines batch-use, tail-batch, used-position, and last-position indicators. Cleaning counts partition each chamber sequence into process segments: $4\times1$ batches are assigned to their cleaning epochs, while a $2\times2$ chain ends its current segment with process-enabling carriers (PECs) when the cleaning threshold is reached and resumes after cleaning with a new PEC-bounded head. Processing assignments, chamber membership, and cleaning boundaries follow from the benchmark configuration; SPBS coordinates dummy occupancy and event times within that layout.

At the resource level, SPBS derives a conservative order from the same physical route. ATR first schedules $4\times1$ inputs and returns across chambers and stages the first $2\times2$ pair of a chamber immediately after that chamber's final full batch. It then advances chain inputs and returns segment by segment. Deterministic odd/even load-lock slots break slot symmetry, and the VTR order is aligned with ATR batch progression and cleaning actions. Let $\mathcal F_q$ be the binary variables fixed in relaxation level $q$, with skeleton value $\widehat x_j$. The restricted feasibility subproblem imposes
\begin{equation}
\ell_j^{(q)}=u_j^{(q)}=\widehat x_j,\qquad j\in\mathcal F_q
\label{eq:spbs-fix-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:spbs-fix-en} fixes only the skeleton choices at the current level. Start, completion, and waiting times, together with resource orders not fixed at that level, remain free.

SPBS progressively relaxes fixed-variable conditions through five levels, with nested fixed sets $\mathcal F_1\supseteq\mathcal F_2\supseteq\cdots\supseteq\mathcal F_5$. For unchanged skeleton values, releasing these conditions expands the restricted feasible set. If a level yields no feasible solution within its allocated budget, the search proceeds to the next level. The first level fixes product and batch assignments, activation states, cleaning segments, load-lock slots, and ATR, AL, LL, and VTR orders. The second releases VTR order; the third also releases load-lock order; the fourth releases all remaining resource orders while retaining structural assignments and slots; and the fifth retains only assignments, activation states, and cleaning segments. For instances with at least 32 wafers, the search starts at the fourth or fifth level. Under a tight waiting-time limit, the original feasible set without additional skeleton restrictions is also considered.

Each level solves a restricted feasibility subproblem with a zero objective and terminates at the first feasible solution. These subproblems use feasibility emphasis, aggressive heuristics, and fast presolve, without cut separation. The resulting principal discrete decisions and continuous event times are then retained in the full model while auxiliary variables associated with schedule stability are completed. A schedule satisfying all original-model constraints becomes the initial incumbent of the main problem. The main problem restores the bounds temporarily tightened by SPBS while retaining the original layout equalities; its feasible set is the complete set conditional on the prescribed layout. In the experiments, SPBS schedules are constructed offline and checked against their corresponding models; the PDI integration interval and main-solve timing both exclude offline initial-schedule construction.

Hierarchical initialization for the joint model starts from a schedule satisfying complete material routes and physical-hand eligibility and improves the skeleton with product makespan as its objective. When enabled, its five levels retain the principal discrete choices, release VTR action orders, release load-lock orders, release the remaining resource orders, and finally retain only structural choices such as pairing, cycle activation, and cycle type. Paired-action synchronization and continuous times remain adjustable at every level. Each level receives at most one fifth of the initialization budget and passes its best complete feasible assignment to the next level. All temporary restrictions are removed before joint main search, which again optimizes pairing, chamber assignment, and resource order. This makespan-improvement initialization differs from the zero-objective feasibility search used for fixed-layout SPBS; construction and main-solve budgets are accounted for separately.

\subsection{HEM baseline and role-aware representation}

Prior learning-based cut selectors address generic candidate sets with reinforcement learning, learned ranking, and look-ahead imitation \citep{tang2020cut,huang2022cutranking,paulus2022looking}. HEM further identifies three coupled decisions---which cuts, how many cuts, and in what order---and handles them with a high-level count policy and a low-level sequence policy \citep{wang2023hem}. We retain HEM's hierarchical decomposition and pointer decoding and use its 13D sequence model as the direct baseline. Training instances, budgets, random seeds, candidate limits, and hidden dimensions are shared while representation, decoding, and post-selection completion are compared. HEM was subsequently extended to hierarchical sequence/set models \citep{wang2024hemset}; HEM-13D here is a controlled reproduction of the 2023 sequence model.

The 13D HEM candidate vector comprises objective parallelism, efficacy, normalized support, integral-support ratio, normalized violation, the mean/maximum/minimum/standard deviation of row coefficients, and the corresponding four objective-coefficient statistics. These features describe immediate LP quality and numerical scale. They do not distinguish two similarly efficacious rows when one acts on discrete route choices and the other on continuous event times. In the present model, that distinction corresponds directly to changing a process/resource combination versus tightening the timing of an existing combination.

Starting from the 13 generic HEM features, variables are partitioned by their mathematical attributes into binary, general integer, objective-active continuous, continuous auxiliary, and other roles. The coefficient mass of role $g$ in cut $i$ is
\begin{equation}
p_{ig}=\frac{\sum_{j:x_j\in g}|a_{ij}|}{\sum_j|a_{ij}|+\varepsilon}.
\label{eq:role-mass-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:role-mass-en} is the fraction of absolute cut-coefficient mass carried by a mathematical variable role. Beyond the five role masses, the added features are mass on variables with two finite global bounds, positive-coefficient mass, discrete--continuous coupling $4(p_{iB}+p_{iI})(p_{iC_{\rm obj}}+p_{iC_{\rm aux}})$, the largest non-residual role mass, and normalized role entropy. The resulting vector has 23 dimensions. Role assignment depends only on variable types, objective coefficients, and global bounds. The representation is therefore invariant to variable renaming that preserves these attributes and the cut coefficients.

HEM uses a hierarchical ``count policy + ordered-subset policy'' decomposition: its high level predicts the number of cuts from a sequence representation, while its low level uses an LSTM encoder--decoder with pointer attention to select cuts sequentially \citep{wang2023hem,vinyals2015pointer}. Building on this hierarchy, the proposed selector introduces a role-aware grouped gated encoder before the LSTM, a role-submodular completion step after the pointer sequence, and width-three beam search during formal inference, which together form the complete RDMCT-HBS selection pipeline.


\subsection{Role-aware encoding and hierarchical sequence decoding}

Let $m_t$ be the number of candidates retained at separation round $t$. For candidate cut $i$, write the 23D input as $x_i=[x_i^{H};x_i^{R}]$, where $x_i^{H}\in\mathbb R^{13}$ contains the generic HEM features and $x_i^{R}\in\mathbb R^{10}$ contains the proposed role-aware features. The full vector is first layer-normalized; the two feature groups are then projected separately into the common hidden space and fused by an elementwise gate generated from all 23 features:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde x_i&=\operatorname{LN}(x_i),\\
u_i^{H}&=W_H\widetilde x_i^{H}+b_H,\qquad
u_i^{R}=W_R\widetilde x_i^{R}+b_R,\\
g_i&=\sigma(W_g\widetilde x_i+b_g),\qquad
z_i=g_i\odot u_i^{H}+(1-g_i)\odot u_i^{R}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:gated-encoding-en}
\end{equation}
Here $z_i\in\mathbb R^{128}$ is the fused candidate embedding. Unlike a single projection of the 23D vector, the two branches preserve generic LP quality and mathematical-role structure separately, while $g_i$ determines their candidate-specific contribution in each hidden dimension. Thus two cuts with similar efficacy but different concentration on discrete route variables or continuous timing variables can remain distinguishable. This grouped gated adapter is a structural change to HEM's input representation introduced in this work.

The fused candidates, in the deterministic order established by Step~1, are passed through a single-layer LSTM encoder:
\begin{equation}
(h_i,c_i)=\operatorname{LSTM}(z_i,h_{i-1},c_{i-1}),\qquad i=1,\ldots,m_t .
\label{eq:lstm-encoding-en}
\end{equation}
The states $h_i$ turn local cut descriptors into context-dependent candidate representations and are used by both the high-level retained-count policy and the low-level ordered selector. A 128D hidden state is used to encode the ordered candidates into a contextual sequence representation. Fixing the input order also makes the encoded sequence reproducible for an identical candidate pool.

At low-level decoding step $r$, the decoder state $d_r$ and all encoder states $h_i$ are used for one glimpse attention followed by pointer attention. Let $M_{ri}=-\infty$ if candidate $i$ already appears in the prefix and $M_{ri}=0$ otherwise. Then
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
a_{ri}&=v_g^\top\tanh(W_e h_i+W_d d_r)+M_{ri},\\
\alpha_{ri}&=\operatorname{softmax}_i(a_{ri}),\\
\bar h_r&=\sum_i\alpha_{ri}h_i,\\
b_{ri}&=v_p^\top\tanh(U_e h_i+U_g\bar h_r)+M_{ri},\\
P(i_r=i\mid i_{1:r-1},S_t)&=\operatorname{softmax}_i(b_{ri}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:pointer-decoding-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:pointer-decoding-en} makes the sequential dependence explicit. The selected prefix updates the decoder state and the next conditional distribution. Masking excludes previously selected cuts, and the glimpse summarizes the candidate pool for the current prefix. The resulting sequence model combines contextual candidate encoding, prefix-aware pointer decoding, and the proposed representation and completion stages into one role-aware selector.

Training keeps stochastic sampling from the policy for exploration. During formal inference, instead of committing to one path at every step, the method maintains $B=3$ partial sequences. For parent beam $b=(i_1,\ldots,i_{r-1})$ and an unselected extension $j$, the accumulated score is
\begin{equation}
L_r(b\oplus j)=L_{r-1}(b)+\log P(i_r=j\mid b,S_t),
\label{eq:beam-score-en}
\end{equation}
All parent-beam--candidate extensions are compared and the three largest $L_r$ values are retained. After the high-level target length $k_t$ is reached, the best sequence is passed to the completion stage. This beam procedure preserves multiple high-probability partial sequences during decoding, reduces early commitment, and works jointly with the subsequent policy-anchored role-submodular completion to inject variable-role structure into the final selected set.

\subsection{Stepwise selection from candidate pool to ordered cuts}

At separation round $t$, the method executes the following sequence.

\noindent\textbf{Step 1: form the candidate pool.} SCIP first generates and validates candidate rows $\mathcal C_t$ with its own separators. To control the candidate-state dimension and evaluation cost, at most 128 candidates are retained in descending order of cut efficacy. Ties are broken by original index for deterministic input. Forced cuts remain under solver control and are not placed in the learned ranking.

\noindent\textbf{Step 2: compute and encode the state.} The 13 HEM and 10 role-aware features are extracted for every row, together with role masses, dominant role, and structural entropy. Equation~\eqref{eq:gated-encoding-en} then performs grouped gated fusion and Eq.~\eqref{eq:lstm-encoding-en} produces contextual candidate states. The encoded candidate set is $S_t$.

\noindent\textbf{Step 3: determine the retained count.} The high-level policy outputs a tanh-bounded action in $[-1,1]$, which is mapped to a retained fraction $\rho_t$. The fraction is then clipped by the global 0.2 cap, SCIP's round-specific limit, and the limit of 16 cuts, producing $k_t$. When enough candidates exist, at least two rows are retained to maintain a nonempty selected subset in each round.

\noindent\textbf{Step 4: construct the candidate order.} At low-level step $r$, the pointer policy conditions on $S_t$ and the selected prefix $i_{1:r-1}$ to score all remaining candidates. Selected indices are masked with negative infinity and cannot be repeated. The joint high- and low-level policy is
\begin{equation}
\pi_\theta(k_t,i_{1:k_t}\mid S_t)=\pi_\theta^H(k_t\mid S_t)
\prod_{r=1}^{k_t}\pi_\theta^L(i_r\mid S_t,i_{1:r-1}).
\label{eq:policy-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:policy-en} means that the high level determines the sequence length and the low level then chooses distinct candidates within that length. Each earlier cut changes both the remaining action set and the decoder state used by later probabilities.

\noindent\textbf{Step 5: beam decoding.} The low-level policy samples stochastically during training. At test time, beam search of width three is used. At step $r$, every partial sequence is extended by every unselected candidate; joint log-probabilities are accumulated, and the three highest-scoring items over all parent-beam--next-candidate pairs are retained. After $k_t$ steps, the best sequence is returned. Unlike one-step greedy decoding, the beam retains an initially lower-ranked prefix whose later joint probability can be higher. This fixed-width neural construction follows the broader use of beam decoding in neural combinatorial optimization \citep{choo2022beam}.

\noindent\textbf{Step 6: role-submodular completion.} A sequence based only on joint probability can still repeatedly cover one variable role. The first $\lceil0.25k_t\rceil$ pointer candidates are retained as policy anchors. The remaining policy order is merged with a heuristic tail sorted by instantaneous structural quality $q_i$, and at most $2k_t$ rows form expansion pool $\mathcal R_t$. For $S\subseteq\mathcal R_t$, define
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
F_t(S)={}&\sum_{i\in S}(0.35q_i+0.20r_i)
+0.25\sum_{g\in\mathcal G}d_g\sqrt{\sum_{i\in S}p_{ig}}\\
&+\frac{0.20}{|\mathcal R_t|}\sum_{u\in\mathcal R_t}\max_{i\in S}s_{ui},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:submodular-en}
\end{equation}
where $r_i$ is the normalized pointer-rank prior, $d_g$ is the demand share of role $g$ in the expansion pool, and $s_{ui}$ is clipped cosine similarity on generic-quality and role-mass descriptors. The score $q_i$ combines efficacy, violation, integral support, recognized-role ratio, dominant-role ratio, and structural entropy. In Eq.~\eqref{eq:submodular-en}, the first term retains instantaneous quality and the learned order; the square-root coverage term gives diminishing marginal gain to repeated role mass; and the final term makes the selected cuts represent the main structures in the pool. Starting from the anchors, the method recomputes the marginal gain of every unselected candidate and adds the largest until $k_t$ cuts have been selected. With nonnegative weights, role masses, and clipped similarities, and $F_t(\varnothing)=0$, the modular, concave-coverage, and facility-location terms form a monotone submodular objective. For fixed anchors $A_t$ and expansion pool, define $f(U)=F_t(A_t\cup U)-F_t(A_t)$. Greedy completion under the cardinality constraint has the standard $1-1/e$ approximation property for this residual objective \citep{nemhauser1978analysis}. This ratio concerns coverage under the residual surrogate objective within the fixed pool; PDI and solution time are evaluated experimentally.

The $k_t$ selected cuts are added to the current LP in anchor order followed by greedy-completion order. Candidate truncation, state encoding, count prediction, sequence decoding, and submodular completion jointly determine the ordered cut set used to strengthen the relaxation in the current round.

\subsection{Policy training and exact solution}

One time-limited SCIP solve forms a rollout, with its terminal PDI used as the cost. The current policy is executed in every eligible cut-selection round. Training uses the high-level fraction and low-level sampled sequence at the first valid candidate set of that rollout as its action sample, and the PDI of the complete solve is returned to both policies. Writing $R=-\pdi$ as a reward, the low-level estimator is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{\nabla_\theta J}
&=\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B(R_b-\bar R_{\rm EMA})
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(\tau_b),\\
\bar R_{\rm EMA}&\leftarrow0.9\bar R_{\rm EMA}+0.1\bar R.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:reinforce-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:reinforce-en} gives the batch policy-gradient update formed from the first eligible candidate-set action sample and an exponential-moving-average baseline; $B$ is the rollout batch and $\tau_b$ is the cut sequence sampled at the first eligible candidate set in rollout $b$. The equivalent loss is PDI-cost advantage times log probability. Low-level log probability is averaged over sequence length so that a longer cut sequence does not produce a larger gradient merely because it contains more terms. The high-level policy uses the same PDI cost and an independent EMA baseline and is updated every two epochs; both gradient norms are clipped at 2. Parallel environments collect rollouts, and a central learner updates both policies synchronously after each batch, forming a parallel hierarchical REINFORCE procedure \citep{williams1992reinforce,mnih2016a3c}.

Training uses five instances and five random seeds. Each seed has two workers, each completing four 30-s/1024-MB rollouts per epoch, for eight rollouts per epoch and 60 epochs. Initial Adam learning rates are $10^{-4}$ for the low-level policy and $5\times10^{-4}$ for the high-level policy and are multiplied by 0.96 every ten epochs. Training uses stochastic decoding and formal inference uses beam width three. Every configuration--seed combination uses the fixed epoch-60 parameters without benchmark- or test-set parameter selection. HEM-13D, feature-only 23D, and the complete 23D method are trained separately so that weights are never mixed across input semantics or completion environments.

\method{} only orders and selects valid rows already generated by SCIP, and SPBS submits only solutions checked in the complete model. Neither changes the objective, constraints, integrality, or bound logic. The learned components therefore change the search path and finite-time bound convergence, whereas feasibility and optimality retain the same meaning as in default SCIP.

\subsection{Experimental design and metrics}

Experiments run on a Windows~11 workstation with an Intel Core Ultra~7 265KF processor, 32~GB RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX~5060~Ti GPU with 8~GB device memory. Mathematical optimization uses SCIP~10.0.1 and PySCIPOpt~6.1.0; policy training and inference use PyTorch~2.11.0, with Python~3.12.12. The fixed-layout benchmark, policy training, and joint-model validation use the same computing environment. Solve budgets and initialization costs are accounted for separately within each comparison family.

The fixed-layout experiments below evaluate prescribed structures under dual-source supply. Section~\ref{sec:joint-evidence-en} separately evaluates joint-model algorithm comparisons, mode sweeps, source routes, and parameter variations. The experiments examine four questions: how lot size and cleaning interval affect mode preference; whether equal-budget two-stage optimization improves waiting and cadence; how SPBS and structure-aware cut selection separately affect feasible-schedule availability and primal--dual convergence; and how scale, composition, and parameter changes affect computational performance. The evidence comprises mode comparisons, the direct HEM comparison, 960 paired SPBS runs, 1500 equal-budget refinement runs, 300 factorial runs, 80 one-factor perturbation runs, and 729 distribution-shift runs across all methods on 40--64-wafer lots. Four resource Gantt charts show both pure modes for 16 wafers at $H=10$ and $H=3$. Every feasible schedule used in the analysis passes SCIP validation against the original model and independent event-time checks.

The compared methods use the same MILP, SCIP version, instances, and random seeds, and each comparison family uses common time and memory limits. Let $P(t)$ denote the primal bound of the minimization problem at time $t$, namely the objective value of the best incumbent, and let $D(t)$ denote the dual bound, namely the global lower bound certified by the search tree. Define $\Delta(t)=|P(t)-D(t)|$, $m(t)=\min\{|P(t)|,|D(t)|\}$, and $M(t)=\max\{|P(t)|,|D(t)|\}$. When the two bounds are finite, have the same sign, and differ, the terminal relative primal--dual gap is defined in SCIP as
\begin{equation}
G_{\rm end}=100\,\frac{\Delta(T)}{m(T)}.
\label{eq:terminal-gap-en}
\end{equation}
SCIP sets $G_{\rm end}=0$ when the bounds coincide and returns $+\infty$ when they have opposite signs or valid bounds are not both available. Equation~\eqref{eq:terminal-gap-en} normalizes by the smaller absolute bound and consequently does not cap $G_{\rm end}$ at 100\%. A value above 100\% indicates that the absolute bound difference exceeds the smaller absolute bound.

SCIP uses a different bounded normalization for the primal--dual integral. Define the instantaneous integral gap as
\begin{equation}
g_{\rm PDI}(t)=100\,\frac{\Delta(t)}{M(t)},\qquad
\pdi=\int_0^T g_{\rm PDI}(t)\,\mathrm dt.
\label{eq:pdi-gap-en}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:pdi-gap-en} applies when the bounds are finite, have the same sign, and differ; SCIP sets $g_{\rm PDI}(t)=0$ for coincident bounds and $g_{\rm PDI}(t)=100$ otherwise. The instantaneous integral gap is therefore bounded to $[0,100]$; lower PDI means that the bound gap closes earlier. PDI is zero only if the gap is zero for almost the entire timed horizon, whereas $G_{\rm end}=0$ only states that optimality is proved at termination. Both quantities follow SCIP definitions and use its solution statistics \citep{achterberg2009scip,bestuzheva2023scip}.

The manufacturing instance is the inferential unit: random repeats are first aggregated within an instance, after which paired instance differences use nonparametric 95\% bootstrap intervals, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, and Holm correction within each prespecified analysis family. Time-limited runs remain in the summaries so that random seeds are not treated as independent manufacturing evidence.

Algebraic representation size and the number of candidate sequences follow different growth rates. For $K_r$ candidate operations on resource $r$, pairwise ordering requires at most $\binom{K_r}{2}$ binary variables, giving an upper bound of $\sum_{r\in\mathcal R}\binom{K_r}{2}$ across resources. \subsection{Search-stage diagnostics with strengthened bounds}

For the dual-source mixed configurations with 8 and 16 products and $H=3$, default SCIP and the complete selector are each run for 120~s with a common feasible initial schedule and identical strengthened bounds. These comprise analytical lower bounds from mandatory routes and resource occupancy and a makespan upper bound from the initial schedule. The four diagnostic runs are separate from the preceding 300-s comparisons and identify the stages consuming the search budget.

For eight products, both methods retain a feasible makespan of 2300~s and terminate with a lower bound of 492~s. Both enter the first root LP solve, executing 25630 and 24675 LP iterations, respectively, before any learned cut-selection call. For 16 products, both retain a feasible makespan of 4221~s and spend the 120-s budget in presolve, with zero processed nodes and zero LP solves. All four final schedules pass independent physical validation. These observations locate the computational effort in presolve and root relaxation, identifying the large discrete formulation and continuous-relaxation solution as the initial search costs for these joint instances.

\subsection{Discussion of mode selection and computational performance}

The clean/dirty-hand joint model coordinates product pairing, dummy allocation, chamber occupancy, and physical-hand assignment. All 107 main runs and seven small-instance structural checks produce complete executable schedules. Resource-order and timing optimization within the original fixed structures improves the four- and six-product makespans from 888 and 1530~s to 532 and 957~s, respectively. Joint search initialized with these schedules retains their principal structures within the tested budget. The dummy-identity comparison instead reduces the four-product makespan to 403~s using the same inventory of eight dummies, with 111~s of concurrent chamber processing. Even with identical product pairing, chamber assignments, and cycle structure, dummy identities can therefore alter attainable concurrency and minimum makespan. Structural selection must account for both product organization and dummy-loan relationships. Equal-budget refinement further quantifies waiting and makespan improvements within a given discrete structure.

Mode comparisons depend on equipment assumptions and solution settings. In the homogeneous-hand fixed-layout benchmark, internal cleaning delays subsequent products, with mixed operation preferred for 16 wafers at $H=4$ and 24 wafers at $H=6$. For 24 wafers at $H=7$--10 in that benchmark, the pure $2\times2$ feasible upper bound of 1237~s lies below both competing optima. The clean/dirty-hand joint model compares schedules under a different set of travel times, hand restrictions, and a 300-s search budget; pure $4\times1$ gives the shortest observed makespan for 24 wafers at every tested cleaning interval. These results respectively support mode selection within prescribed layouts and evaluation of schedules obtained by joint search.

Solver components affect different performance measures. Precomputed SPBS improves feasible-schedule availability during time-limited fixed-layout search, and two-stage refinement reduces waiting and improves cadence. With common SPBS, the complete selector and HEM have close observed mean metrics. The clean/dirty-hand experiments further show that initialization supplies an executable starting point for joint scheduling, timing refinement improves the selected structure, and learned cut selection acts only once separation is reached. Feasible-schedule availability, timing quality, and bound convergence therefore require separate evaluation.

\section{Conclusions}

This study develops a two-stage joint scheduling model for PECVD rotating-chamber cluster tools. Wafer pairing connects product and dummy supply/return routes, while chamber assignment, resource order, physical-hand assignment, and event times jointly represent full occupancy, dummy reuse, periodic cleaning, and residency constraints. Single- and dual-source configurations differ in the origin and route of dummy wafers, and clean/dirty-hand eligibility applies throughout each holding interval.

All 107 main runs and seven small-instance structural checks of the clean/dirty-hand model produce independently validated physical schedules. For four products, reassigning dummy identities and reoptimizing timing while preserving product pairing, chamber assignments, cycle structure, and the eight-dummy inventory reduces makespan from the original fixed-structure optimum of 532~s to 403~s, a reduction of 24.25\%, with 111~s of concurrent chamber processing. Dummy-loan relationships are thus substantive structural decisions affecting attainable concurrency and makespan. Across six single- and dual-source configurations under the same 300-s total solve budget, two-stage refinement reduces total transport-route waiting by 8.40\%--29.21\%, decreases maximum individual waiting from 73 to 65~s, and shortens actual product makespan in every case. The 72 mode-sweep cells for lots of 8, 16, and 24 wafers characterize feasible schedules under each cleaning condition; pure $4\times1$ gives the shortest observed makespan for 24 wafers throughout the tested $H=3$--10 range. Eight small source-route schedules additionally realize product--dummy pairing, cross-LP pairing of same-recipe products, and both supply/return routes with valid clean/dirty-hand assignments.

The homogeneous-hand fixed-layout benchmark separately quantifies mode selection and solver components. Under equal cumulative nominal processing time, lot size and cleaning position jointly change mode preference. For 16 wafers without cleaning, the pure $2\times2$ makespan is 898~s, 4.87\% below pure $4\times1$. Under the same 600-s total budget, two-stage refinement reduces total route waiting by 51.91\%. Precomputed SPBS raises feasible-schedule availability in the subsequent main solve from 51.67\% to 100\% and reduces mean PDI by 26.19\%. Together with the clean/dirty-hand experiments, these results support joint coordination of dummy supply/return, cleaning, and product processing, with structured initialization and continuous-time refinement contributing to schedule construction and operating quality, respectively.
